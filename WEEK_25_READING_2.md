# THE BIRTH PARTNER'S FIELD GUIDE
### *Read this in the parking lot. Read it again in the hallway. You'll be fine. She'll be fine. Here's what's actually coming.*

---

A note before we start: You're nervous. Good. That means you care. I've walked thousands of partners through this door, and I'll tell you what I tell all of them — **you do not need to be calm. You need to be *present*.** Those are different things. You can be scared and still be exactly what she needs. Now let's get you ready.

---

## PART 1: WHAT YOU WILL ACTUALLY SEE, HEAR, AND FEEL

Forget the movies. Forget the TV shows where she screams once and the baby's out. Here's the real sensory experience, stage by stage, as the person standing three feet away.

### EARLY LABOR (0–6 cm) — *The Long Warm-Up*
**Duration:** 6–12+ hours. Sometimes days of "is this it?" before this.

- **What you'll see:** She'll be *almost* normal. Talking. Maybe making jokes. Then a contraction hits and she'll pause mid-sentence, look slightly inward, maybe press a hand to her lower belly or the small of her back. Her face tightens for 30–45 seconds. Then it passes and she's back. "Okay. That was one."
- **What you'll hear:** Quiet breathing. Maybe a low "hmm" through her nose. She can still talk to you during a contraction, though she might not want to.
- **What you'll feel:** Boredom, honestly. And anxiety. You'll keep asking "should we go in yet?" She'll probably say no. **Let her decide.** Early labor at home is a gift. Make her food. Fill the tub. Dim the lights. Don't hover.
- **The thing nobody tells you:** She may get irritable with you for no reason. She's turning inward. Her brain is shifting into a different gear. Don't take it personally. Make a sandwich and back off.

### ACTIVE LABOR (6–8 cm) — *The Shift*
**Duration:** 3–6 hours typically.

- **What you'll see:** The change is unmistakable. She can no longer talk through a contraction. She'll stop what she's doing, close her eyes, grip the bed rail or your forearm or the back of a chair. Her whole body tenses — shoulders rise, jaw clenches, toes curl. Contractions are now 45–60 seconds, 3–5 minutes apart. Between them, she's not chatty anymore. She's *resting*, eyes half-closed, breathing hard.
- **What you'll hear:** Moaning. Low, guttural sounds. "Ahhhh" or "Ohhh" that she can't suppress. She may say "that one was stronger." She may ask for water, then push it away. She might say "I don't like this." **That's normal. She's telling you she's in it, not that something's wrong.**
- **What you'll feel:** Your helplessness will spike here. You'll want to fix it. **You can't fix it. You can only walk through it beside her.** This is the stage where your physical work begins — counter-pressure on her sacrum, hip squeezes, a cold washcloth on her forehead. (More on this in Part 2.)

### TRANSITION (8–10 cm) — *The Storm. The Shortest, Hardest Part.*
**Duration:** 20 minutes to 2 hours. It feels like forever. It is not.

- **What you'll see:** This is the part that scares partners, so I'm going to be very clear: **she will look like she's losing control, and that is exactly what's supposed to happen.** She may vomit. She may shake violently — legs, arms, whole body, like a fever chill. Her face will flush, then go pale. She'll grip you hard enough to bruise. She may cry. She may say **"I can't do this. I can't. I want to go home. I want an epidural. I can't do this anymore."**
- **What you'll hear:** Louder vocalization. Grunting, groaning, maybe a sound you've never heard her make — animal, raw, involuntary. She may swear. She may say "get away from me" and then grab your hand ten seconds later. Contractions are 60–90 seconds, 2–3 minutes apart, and they *stack* — she barely gets a breath between them.
- **What you'll feel:** Fear. You'll think *something is wrong.* You'll think *I should call someone.* **Unless there's a medical red flag (see Part 5), this is the most normal thing in the world.** Transition means she's almost there. The cervix is finishing. When she says "I can't do this," what she actually means is **"I am doing this and it is overwhelming."** Your job is to get in her line of sight, make eye contact, and say: **"You ARE doing it. You are right in the middle of it. And it's almost over. I'm right here."** Do not say "you're doing great." Say "you're doing it." There's a difference. One is a judgment. The other is a fact.
- **The thing nobody tells you:** Transition is the *shortest* stage. When it feels the worst, it's the closest to being over. Hold that thought.

### PUSHING (Second Stage) — *The Work*
**Duration:** 20 minutes to 3+ hours (longer with an epidural, shorter if she's pushed before).

- **What you'll see:** A visible, physical shift. Her face changes — she looks *determined*, almost angry. She'll tuck her chin, grab her thighs or the bed handles, and *bear down* with her whole body. You'll see her abdominal muscles contract. Her face will redden. She may grunt loudly, hold her breath, push for 6–10 seconds, release, breathe, push again. Between contractions she'll slump back, exhausted.
- **Crowning:** You will see the top of the baby's head appear at the opening, then slip back, then appear again, a little more each time. The nurse or midwife will say "I see hair" or "the head is crowning." She may feel a burning, stretching sensation — the "ring of fire." She may yell. The head delivers. Then the shoulders. Then the baby slides out, wet, grayish-purple, slick.
- **What you'll hear:** Grunting. Straining sounds. The nurse/midwife coaching: "Push! Push! Okay, stop, pant, pant, pant." Then — the cry. Or sometimes a few seconds of silence before the cry. **A few seconds of silence is normal.** Don't panic.
- **What you'll feel:** If you're at the head of the bed (and I recommend you are), you'll see her face, not the delivery. You'll see her push, rest, push. You'll hear the baby before you see it. And then they'll put the baby on her chest and she'll look up at you and you will both be wrecked. That's the plan.

### THIRD STAGE (Placenta) — *The Afterthought*
**Duration:** 5–30 minutes.

- She'll have one or two more mild contractions. The placenta delivers. She may not even notice. She's holding the baby. You're holding the baby. Someone is checking her for tearing. It's anticlimactic and beautiful. She may shiver uncontrollably for 20 minutes. That's normal. Get her a warm blanket.

## PART 2: YOUR TOP 5 JOBS, RANKED

Not "hold her hand." Not "look supportive." Actual jobs with actual impact.

### **#1: BE THE PHYSICAL COMFORT ENGINE**
This is your single most important role. You are her counter-pressure machine.
- **Double hip squeeze:** During a contraction, stand behind her, place the heels of your hands on the bony points of her hips (the iliac crests), and squeeze *inward and slightly upward* with steady, firm pressure. Ask "harder?" It will almost always be "harder." You cannot squeeze too hard.
- **Sacral counter-pressure:** Find the flat triangular bone at the base of her spine (the sacrum). Press the heel of your hand or a tennis ball firmly into it during contractions. This directly counters the back pain.
- **Cold cloth:** Forehead, back of neck. Replace it often.
- **Warm compress:** On her lower back or perineum during pushing (the nurse can hand you one).
- **Position changes:** Help her move. Hands-and-knees. Leaning forward over the bed. Squatting. Side-lying. She should not be flat on her back for hours. Suggest movement every 20–30 minutes in active labor.

### **#2: BE THE TIMEKEEPER AND PATTERN-READER**
- Time contractions from the *start* of one to the *start* of the next. Note duration (how long each one lasts). Use a phone app or a watch. Write it down.
- You will report this to the nurse: "Contractions are 4 minutes apart, lasting 60 seconds, and they've been that way for an hour."
- Watch for the *pattern shift* — when she can no longer talk, when she starts vocalizing, when she turns inward. These tell you more than the clock.

### **#3: BE THE GATEKEEPER**
- **Manage the room.** Dim the lights. Reduce noise. If her mother/aunt/friend is talking too much, *you* are the one who says, "She needs quiet right now. Let's step into the hallway." She cannot do this. She is in labor. You can.
- **Limit visitors.** One or two support people max. You are allowed to say no.
- **Control the temperature.** Labor rooms are cold. She'll be hot. Have a fan. Have a blanket. Adjust without being asked.
- **Protect her space during vaginal exams.** Step in front of her, make eye contact, say "You okay with this?" before the nurse proceeds. She may be too far gone to object.

### **#4: BE THE ADVOCATE** *(See Part 4 for exact scripts.)*
- She will reach a point in active labor and transition where she *cannot* process information, ask questions, or say no. That's when you speak. Not to override her. To make sure her previously stated wishes are honored and that she gets the information she needs to consent.

### **#5: BE THE EMOTIONAL ANCHOR**
- Stay in the room. If you must leave (bathroom, food), tell her: "I'm going to the bathroom. I'll be back in two minutes. [Name/doula/nurse] is here with you."
- Get in her eyeline during contractions. Breathe *with* her. Exaggerate your breathing so she can follow it. Low, slow: "Breathe in... and out... in... and out."
- Touch her. Hand on her shoulder. Fingers in her hair. Steady, grounding contact. Not fidgety. Not anxious. *Steady.*
- Say: "You're doing it." "I'm here." "This contraction will end." "The next one will come and the next one will end too."
- **Do NOT say:** "You're doing great" (she doesn't feel great), "Almost there" (you don't know), "Calm down" (never), or "Is there anything I can do?" (she can't answer that; just *do* the things in Job #1).

## PART 3: THE 3 BIGGEST MISTAKES PARTNERS MAKE

### **Mistake #1: Taking Her Pain Personally and Trying to "Fix" It**
You see her suffering and your brain screams *do something, make it stop.* You offer seventeen suggestions. "Do you want water? A pillow? Should I call the nurse? Do you want to try—?" She snaps at you. You feel rejected.

**Instead:** You cannot fix labor. You can *ease* it. Counter-pressure. Cool cloth. Breathe with her. Do one thing at a time. Do it for two full contractions before switching. Stop asking questions. Start doing. If she pushes your hand away, try the other hip. If she says "stop," stop. If she says nothing, keep doing what you're doing. **Her labor is not a problem you solve. It's a process you support.**

### **Mistake #2: Freezing or Disappearing When It Gets Intense**
Transition hits. She vomits. She screams. She grabs your arm and says "I hate you, get away from me, I can't do this." And you freeze. Or you step back. Or you look at the nurse with a *what do I do* face. Or you leave the room "to get air."

**Instead:** She does not hate you. She is in the hardest physical experience of her life and you are the safest person to say that to. **Do not leave.** Step slightly back if she needs space, but stay in the room. Get the emesis basin. Wipe her face. When the contraction passes, get back in. Say: "I know. I know it's awful. You're doing it. I'm not going anywhere." If you need to cry, do it in the hallway for 60 seconds, then come back. She needs your *presence* more than your composure.

### **Mistake #3: Arguing With Her or Negotiating During Labor**
She says "I want an epidural" at 4 cm. You say "But you said you wanted to go unmedicated! You can do this!" Or she says "I want to go home" during transition and you say "We can't, the baby's coming."

**Instead:** You are not the birth plan police. Her plan is a *preference*, not a contract. If she asks for pain relief, you say: "Okay. I'll ask the nurse what's available." If she wants to go home during transition, you say: "I hear you. Let me ask the nurse where you are." Then you get the information and *she* decides. Your job is to make sure she's making informed choices, not to enforce a document she wrote at 34 weeks. **Her body. Her call. Every time.**

## PART 4: ADVOCATING FOR HER — EXACT PHRASES

You are not being "difficult." You are not being "that husband." You are making sure a woman in the most vulnerable physical state of her life gets the information and respect she's entitled to. Here are scripts. Memorize three of them.

**When a procedure is proposed and she hasn't consented:**
> "Before you proceed, can you explain to *her* what you're recommending, why, and what the alternatives are? She'd like to make that decision herself."

**When you feel rushed:**
> "She's not ready to decide yet. Can we have ten minutes to talk it over? Is this an emergency *right now*, or do we have time?"

*(This is the key question. "Is this an emergency right now?" If the answer is no, you have time. If the answer is yes, you listen.)*

**When someone addresses you instead of her:**
> "I appreciate that, but please direct that question to her. She's the patient."

**When her stated wishes are being overridden:**
> "Her birth plan says she'd like to avoid [intervention] unless it's medically necessary. Can you walk us through why it's necessary in this specific situation?"

**When you need a second opinion or a pause:**
> "I'd like to speak with the attending/charge nurse before we proceed. Can we pause for a moment?"

**When you need people to leave the room:**
> "She needs fewer people in here right now. Can everyone except [name] step out, please?"

**The nuclear option — if you feel she is not being heard and it's not an emergency:**
> "I need to be clear: she is not consenting to this. Can you document in her chart that we've declined, and explain what the risks of declining are?"

**One rule:** Be firm. Be polite. Be *specific.* "I don't feel comfortable" is weak. "She has not consented to an episiotomy. Please do not perform one without her explicit verbal consent" is strong. Use her name. Use the word "consent." Use the word "medically necessary." These are not aggressive. They are *precise.*

## PART 5: NORMAL PAIN vs. SOMETHING IS WRONG

This is the question that will keep you up at 2 a.m. before the birth. Here's your quick-reference.

### **THIS IS NORMAL — DO NOT PANIC:**
- ✅ Rhythmic contractions that build, peak, and fade. She can breathe through them (even if loudly).
- ✅ She's vocalizing — moaning, groaning, grunting. **Silence is more concerning than noise.**
- ✅ She says "I can't do this" during transition. (She can. She is.)
- ✅ Vomiting during transition. Shaking. Crying. Swearing. All normal.
- ✅ A small amount of bloody-tinged mucus ("bloody show") or pink-tinged fluid.
- ✅ The baby's heart rate on the monitor dips during a contraction and recovers after. (The nurse is watching this. You don't need to.)
- ✅ She's exhausted, sleepy, "out of it" between contractions. She's conserving energy.
- ✅ She pushes and nothing seems to happen for a while. First babies can take 1–3 hours of pushing.

### **SOMETHING MAY BE WRONG — GET A NURSE OR PROVIDER NOW:**
- 🚨 **Constant, unrelenting pain** that does NOT come in waves. A contraction should peak and release. If her abdomen is rigid and the pain doesn't stop, press the call button.
- 🚨 **Bright red vaginal bleeding** — not pink-tinged, not mucus-streaked. *Red. Flowing. Like a period or more.* Tell the nurse immediately. ("She's having bright red bleeding.")
- 🚨 **Her water breaks and the fluid is green, brown, or thick.** (Clear or pale straw-colored is normal. Green/brown = meconium. Tell the nurse.)
- 🚨 **She has a fever over 100.4°F / 38°C**, especially with chills or a racing heart.
- 🚨 **The baby's heart rate on the monitor drops and does NOT recover** after the contraction. You'll see the nurse's demeanor change. They'll reposition her, give oxygen, maybe call the doctor. **If they don't notice, say: "The heart rate looks like it dropped. Can someone check?"**
- 🚨 **She says "something feels wrong."** Not "this hurts." Not "I can't do this." A calm, specific, *different* statement: "Something is wrong." **Listen to her. Always. Get a nurse.**
- 🚨 **She becomes unresponsive, confused, slurred, or has a severe headache with visual changes** (seeing spots, blinding light). This could be preeclampsia/eclampsia. Get help *now.*
- 🚨 **More than 2 hours of pushing with no progress** and the provider isn't addressing it. Ask: "What's the plan? What are we watching for? At what point do we reassess?"

### **The Simple Rule:**
> *Pain that comes and goes in a rhythm = labor. Pain that is constant, or bleeding that is bright red, or a gut feeling from her that "this is different" = call someone. When in doubt, press the call button. You will never be yelled at for pressing the call button.*

## PART 6: THE NIGHT-BEFORE CHECKLIST

- [ ] Phone charged. Charger packed.
- [ ] Her birth plan: 3 printed copies (one for you, one for the nurse, one for the chart).
- [ ] Snacks for you. You will forget to eat. Granola bars, jerky, nuts, a banana.
- [ ] Water bottle. For her AND for you.
- [ ] Change of clothes for you. You will get sweaty. Possibly vomited on.
- [ ] A small speaker if she wants music. A phone playlist she made at 36 weeks.
- [ ] Lip balm for her. Her mouth will be dry.
- [ ] A hair tie. A cold washcloth in a ziplock bag.
- [ ] Know where the hospital parking is. Know the L&D floor number. Know the check-in process. Do NOT figure this out at 2 a.m. while she's having contractions.
- [ ] **Sleep.** Even if you can't. Lie down. Close your eyes. You'll need it.

## ONE LAST THING

You're going to walk into that room and think: *I have no idea what I'm doing.*

You do. You just read this guide. You know what's coming. You know what to do with your hands, your voice, and your presence. You know the phrases. You know the red flags.

And here's what I've seen in forty-plus years of attending births: **the partner who shows up, stays in the room, breathes with her, presses on her hips, and says "I'm here, you're doing it" — that partner is enough.** You don't need to be a doula. You don't need to be a doctor. You need to be the one person in that room who is not performing a medical role, who is simply *hers.* The one who looks at her and says, with their whole face: *I see you. I'm not going anywhere. You are not alone in this.*

She will remember how you showed up. Not the words. Not the technique. The fact that you stayed, you breathed, you pressed your hands into her hips and *held on* while she did the hardest thing she'll ever do.

You've got this. She's got this. You've got each other.

Now go make her a sandwich, fill the tub, and get some sleep.

— *Penny*

# THE PITOCIN CONVERSATION: A SCRIPT
### *Or: "They Want to Speed Things Up and She Wants to Wait. Here's Exactly What You Say."*

---

A note before the script: I'm going to write this as a *real* conversation, with the nurse's lines included, so you can recognize the pattern when it happens. Because it will happen. Roughly 1 in 3 labors involve Pitocin augmentation, and the conversation often comes when she's tired, 4–6 cm, and things have slowed. You'll be at hour nine. She'll be dozing between contractions. And someone in scrubs will walk in, glance at the monitor, and say the words.

You are not the enemy of this nurse. She is not the enemy of your wife. She is doing her job, which includes flagging slow progress. Your job is to make sure the *decision* is unhurried, informed, and **hers.**

One more thing: **Pitocin is sometimes genuinely the right call.** Ruptured membranes for 18+ hours. Signs of infection. Baby's heart rate trending down. A true stall at 7+ cm. I'm not here to tell you to refuse it. I'm here to make sure you don't say yes in thirty seconds because a person in authority walked into the room and your wife was too exhausted to process what was being proposed.

You're buying *time to decide.* Not refusing care. There's a difference, and the scripts below reflect it.

---

## THE SCENE

*She's 5 cm. Contractions were 4 minutes apart and now they're 7–8. She's been 5 cm for about two hours. She's tired. She's been dozing. The nurse checks the monitor, checks the clock, and says:*

**NURSE:** "So, I talked with Dr. [Name]. Progress has slowed down a bit. You've been at five for a while. We'd like to start some Pitocin to get things moving. I can get that going in about ten minutes."

---

## BEAT 1: DON'T SAY YES OR NO. ASK QUESTIONS.

*Your first words are not "okay" and not "no." They are questions. Calm voice. Not confrontational. You're gathering information.*

**YOU:** "Okay. Thank you for letting us know. Before we decide anything — can I ask a few questions?"

*(This is not permission-seeking. You're telling her you're going to ask questions. The tone is: "I'm engaged, I'm listening, and I need information." Most nurses will respect this immediately.)*

**YOU:** "Is this a *medical* concern right now — like, is the baby's heart rate okay, is her temperature okay, is there a reason we can't wait? Or is this more about the pace of progress?"

> **Why you ask this:** This is the single most important question in the entire conversation. The answer changes everything.
>
> - If she says *"Baby's heart rate is fine, mom's vitals are fine, we just want to see more cervical change"* — you have time. This is a *preference for speed*, not an emergency.
> - If she says *"Her water broke fourteen hours ago and we're watching for infection"* or *"The baby's heart rate has been dipping and not recovering well"* — this is a medical conversation. You listen. You ask follow-ups. You may well say yes. And that's okay.

**YOU:** "Can you explain what 'slow progress' means here specifically? What were you hoping to see, and what are you seeing?"

> **Why:** "Slow" is relative. ACOG (the American College of Obstetricians) updated guidelines in 2014: active labor is now considered to begin at **6 cm**, not 4. A first-time mom can sit at 4–5 cm for *many* hours and that can be completely normal. You want to know: is she at 5 cm and they expected 7 by now? Or is she at 5 and they're comparing her to a textbook curve from 1955? You don't need to argue the guidelines. You just need to hear their reasoning.

**YOU:** "What would happen if we waited another hour — or two — before starting Pitocin? What are you watching for in that window?"

> **Why:** This is the time-buy. You're not saying no. You're saying *"not yet."* And you're asking them to name the specific threshold. If the answer is "we'd just like to see her progress to six," you can often get that time. If the answer is "we're concerned about infection risk because her membranes ruptured at 2 a.m.," the calculus changes.

---

## BEAT 2: ASK ABOUT ALTERNATIVES

**YOU:** "Before we go to Pitocin, are there other things we can try first to help contractions pick back up? Walking, position changes, getting in the tub, nipple stimulation? Is there a reason those wouldn't work here?"

> **Why:** Sometimes labor stalls because she's been lying in a bed for three hours. Sometimes the baby's position is slightly off and a hands-and-knees position or a hip tilt changes everything. Sometimes she's dehydrated or exhausted and needs IV fluids and a nap, not oxytocin. You're not being anti-medical. You're asking: *"Have we exhausted the low-intervention options?"* A good nurse will say, "Sure, let's try walking for thirty minutes." A rushed one might wave it off. Push gently.

**YOU:** "If we try [walking / the tub / position changes] for forty-five minutes and her contractions don't pick up, can we revisit the Pitocin conversation then?"

> **Why:** This is the *structured time-buy.* You're giving the nurse a concrete plan and a concrete timeline. You're not being obstructionist. You're saying: *"We'll try X. If it doesn't work, we'll talk again."* This is very hard for a provider to say no to, because it's reasonable, it's time-limited, and it keeps them in the loop. Write down the time. "Okay, it's 3:15. Let's reassess at 4:00."

---

## BEAT 3: TURN TO YOUR WIFE

*This is the part most partners skip. They negotiate with the nurse and forget the person in the bed. Don't do that.*

*Lean in. Get in her eyeline. Lower your voice. Speak to her, not the nurse.*

**YOU:** *(to her, quietly)* "Hey. Look at me. The nurse is suggesting Pitocin to speed up contractions. I asked if it's urgent and [summarize the answer in one sentence]. I asked if we can wait an hour and try walking first. **What do you want?** You don't have to decide right this second. I'm handling the conversation. You just tell me what you want."

> **Why:** She is exhausted. She is in pain. She may be in that foggy, inward place where processing a medical decision feels impossible. Your job is to *translate* the situation into two sentences and then hand her the decision. Not "what should we do?" — that's too big. **"What do you want?"** And then: **"I'll talk to them. You rest."**

*She might say "I want to wait." She might say "I want the Pitocin, I'm so tired." She might say "I don't know." All three are valid.*

- If she says **"I want to wait"**: You go back to the nurse and advocate. (Beat 4.)
- If she says **"I want the Pitocin"**: You say okay. You ask the nurse to explain the dose, the monitoring, what to expect. You don't override her to protect some birth plan. (More on this below.)
- If she says **"I don't know"**: You say, *"That's okay. I'm going to ask them to give us thirty minutes. You don't have to decide right now. I'll stay right here."* Then you buy the time.

---

## BEAT 4: GOING BACK TO THE NURSE (SHE WANTS TO WAIT)

**YOU:** *(to the nurse, calm, firm, not aggressive)* "She'd like to wait. She wants to try [walking / the tub / position changes] for the next forty-five minutes to an hour before making a decision about Pitocin. I'd like to document that we've discussed it and that we're choosing to wait and try those measures first. Can we reassess at [specific time]?"

*If the nurse pushes back:*

**NURSE:** "I understand, but Dr. [Name] really wants to get things moving. I'd need to call and let them know you're declining."

**YOU:** "That's fine. Please call. And when you do, can you ask them specifically: *Is there a medical reason we cannot wait one more hour?* Not a scheduling reason. Not a preference. A medical reason. And can you relay the answer back to us?"

> **Why this works:** You're not saying "the doctor is wrong." You're asking for the *medical justification* to be stated clearly. Most of the time, the answer is "no, an hour is fine." Sometimes the answer is "yes, because of X," and then you have real information to bring back to your wife.

*If the nurse says "I can't really override the doctor's orders":*

**YOU:** "I understand you're relaying the doctor's recommendation, and I respect that. But she is the patient, and she has the right to decline or delay a non-emergency intervention. I'm not asking you to override anyone. I'm asking for **one hour** to try non-pharmacological measures, and then we'll revisit. If at that point progress hasn't changed, we'll have the Pitocin conversation again. Can you support that?"

> **Why this works:** You named the word: *"the patient."* You named the right: *"decline or delay."* You gave a concrete, reasonable timeline. You were respectful. You didn't raise your voice. You didn't say "you can't make us." You said: *"She has the right, and here's a reasonable middle path."* This is very, very hard to argue with.

*If you need to escalate:*

**YOU:** "I'd like to speak with the charge nurse, please. And if the doctor is available, I'd like them to come speak with *her* directly, not relay through the nurse. She'd like to hear the recommendation and the reasoning from the provider before she consents."

> This is not a threat. This is a reasonable request. The charge nurse exists for exactly this. And a provider who recommends an intervention should be willing to explain it to the patient face-to-face.

---

## BEAT 5: IF THE ANSWER IS "YES, THIS IS MEDICALLY NECESSARY"

*Maybe the nurse comes back and says: "The doctor is concerned because her water broke sixteen hours ago and her temperature is creeping up. We really need to get this baby delivered in a reasonable window."*

*Or: "The baby's heart rate has had two decelerations that took a while to recover. We want to get things moving."*

**You do not dig in your heels.** You turn to your wife.

**YOU:** *(to her)* "The doctor is saying [one-sentence summary]. They're saying it's not just about speed — there's a medical reason. I want you to hear that. Do you want them to come explain it to you directly? Do you have questions? **You can still say yes or no. But I want you to have the information.**"

*If she says yes to Pitocin:*

**YOU:** *(to the nurse)* "Okay. She consents to starting Pitocin. Can you walk us through what that looks like — the starting dose, how you'll increase it, what the monitoring will be, and what side effects to watch for? And can we still do position changes and movement once the IV is in, or does she need to stay in bed?"

> **Why:** Pitocin doesn't mean she's strapped to the bed. Many hospitals allow walking with a portable monitor. Ask. Also: Pitocin contractions are *different.* They come on faster, peak harder, and don't let you breathe between them the same way. Warn her: *"The contractions are going to feel different. Stronger. Faster. I'm going to be right here with the hip squeezes. We'll get through it."* And then you do.

---

## BEAT 6: WHAT NOT TO SAY

- ❌ *"We don't want any drugs."* (Too broad. Too adversarial. You're not anti-medicine. You're pro-information.)
- ❌ *"You can't make us."* (True, but it escalates. You don't need to win a power struggle. You need an hour.)
- ❌ *"I read online that Pitocin causes [X]."* (Don't litigate the internet in the room. Ask *this* provider about *this* patient's *specific* situation.)
- ❌ *(To your wife, in front of the nurse):* *"But you said you didn't want interventions!"* (Do NOT do this. Her preferences can evolve. She is allowed to change her mind. She is allowed to be tired. You are not the birth-plan police. See Mistake #3 in the field guide.)
- ❌ Saying "yes" immediately because the nurse walked in and you froze. **You are always allowed to say: "Can I have a minute to talk with her?"**

| You need to escalate. | "I'd like the charge nurse, please." |

## THE UNDERLYING PRINCIPLE

You are not fighting the medical system. You are doing one thing:

**Making sure a tired, laboring woman gets the information she needs, the time she needs, and the respect to make her own decision about her own body.**

That's it. That's the whole job in this conversation. You're not a lawyer. You're not a doctor. You're the person who makes sure nobody steamrolls her because she's exhausted and in pain and a person in authority walked into the room with a clipboard.

You ask the questions. You buy the hour. You turn to her and say *"What do you want?"* And then you do what she says.

You've got this. She's got this.

Now go put your phone on silent, fill her water bottle, and get back in that room.

— *Penny*

# THE PARTNER'S SURVIVAL GUIDE: EATING, SLEEPING, AND NOT COLLAPSING AT HOUR 14
### *Or: "You Are No Use to Her If You Pass Out in the Hallway."*

---

Here's what nobody tells you about a 12-to-20-hour labor: **the hardest physical stretch for *you* is not the pushing. It's hours 10 through 15.** She's in active labor or transition. You've been on your feet since 2 a.m. You had a granola bar at 6 and a cold coffee at 9. Your lower back aches from hunching over the bed doing hip squeezes. Your phone is at 11%. Your hands are cramping from counter-pressure. And she needs you to be *steady* for the next three hours.

I have seen partners faint. I have seen them vomit in the trash can (not the emesis basin — the *trash can*). I have seen them sit down in the hallway and quietly cry because they forgot to eat for nine hours and their blood sugar cratered. And I have seen the ones who packed a bag, ate on a schedule, and sat down when they could — and those partners were *present* for the whole thing. Clear-eyed. Steady-handed. There.

You are an endurance athlete for the next 20 hours. Train and fuel like one. Here's how.

---

## PART 1: THE PACKING LIST (FOR YOU, NOT HER)

Pack this the night before. Put it in a backpack, not a duffel — you need your hands free.

### FOOD & FUEL
- [ ] **6–8 snack items you can eat one-handed, standing up, in a hallway.** (See Part 2 for specifics.)
- [ ] **A real meal or two.** A wrapped sandwich. A container of pasta. Something with actual calories that isn't a vending machine Pop-Tart. You will eat this during early labor when there's time to sit.
- [ ] **Electrolyte packets or a sports drink.** Two or three. Gatorade, Liquid I.V., coconut water — whatever you'll actually drink. You will sweat more than you expect.
- [ ] **A large water bottle.** 32 oz minimum. Refill it every time you pass a water fountain. Set a mental rule: *every time she has a contraction, I take a sip.* You'll drink without thinking about it.
- [ ] **Caffeine — but the right kind and the right time.** (More in Part 3.)
- [ ] **Gum or mints.** You will be six inches from her face for hours. Coffee breath and stale-cracker breath are not what she needs while she's trying to breathe through a 90-second contraction.

### CLOTHES & BODY
- [ ] **A full change of clothes.** Shirt, underwear, socks. You will get sweaty. You may get vomited on. There may be blood. You will feel human again after you change. Pack it in a ziplock bag so it stays clean.
- [ ] **Comfortable shoes. Broken-in sneakers.** Not new shoes. Not flip-flops. Not dress shoes. You will be standing, pacing, squatting beside the bed, leaning against walls. Your feet will hate you by hour 12 if you wear the wrong shoes.
- [ ] **Layers.** A t-shirt, a hoodie or flannel, a light jacket. Hospital rooms swing between arctic and tropical. You'll be hot from stress and cold from the AC. Zip, unzip, zip, unzip.
- [ ] **Deodorant. Toothbrush and toothpaste. Face wipes.** You will feel gross by hour 10. A 3-minute bathroom trip to brush your teeth and wipe your face will make you feel like a person again. Do it.

### GEAR
- [ ] **Phone charger AND a portable battery pack.** Your phone will die. You will use it for contraction timing, calls to family, bad hospital Wi-Fi, and 2 a.m. Googling "is this normal" (it is). A dead phone at hour 8 is a small crisis you don't need.
- [ ] **Cash — small bills.** Vending machines. Parking garage. The cafeteria at 3 a.m. when nothing else is open. Some hospital gift shops don't take Apple Pay at 4 a.m.
- [ ] **Earbuds.** Not for music during labor — for a 20-minute nap in the family room while a doula or her mom sits with her. You need to be able to hear if someone calls your name, but you also need to block out the fluorescent hum and the TV on the wall.
- [ ] **A small pillow or a rolled-up hoodie.** For the aforementioned nap. For leaning against a hard plastic chair. For putting under your lower back when you finally sit down.
- [ ] **A book, a downloaded show, a podcast.** For early labor. The first 6–10 hours may be *boring.* She's at home or in triage, contractions are manageable, and you're sitting in a chair watching her scroll her phone. You need something to keep your brain from spiraling into anxiety. Download it the night before. Hospital Wi-Fi is unreliable.
- [ ] **A pen and a small notebook.** For contraction timing if your phone dies. For writing down questions for the doctor. For the birth story details you'll want later: "First contraction: 11:40 p.m. Water broke: 3:15 a.m. Baby born: 2:47 p.m." You will forget. Write it down.

### PAPERWORK
- [ ] **Her ID, insurance card, hospital pre-registration confirmation.** (Yes, this is "her" stuff, but *you* are the one who will be handing it to the admission clerk while she's having a contraction in the wheelchair.)
- [ ] **3 printed copies of the birth plan.** One for you. One for the nurse. One for the chart.
- [ ] **Your hospital bag checklist, printed.** At 2 a.m., with contractions 6 minutes apart, you will forget the phone charger. The list will not.

## PART 2: WHAT TO EAT AND WHEN

The rule is simple and non-negotiable: **Eat before you are hungry. Drink before you are thirsty. You will not notice either until it's too late.**

Adrenaline suppresses appetite. You will feel "fine" at hour 6 and then hit a wall at hour 11 where your hands shake, your vision goes slightly gray, and you can't remember the last thing you ate. That's a blood sugar crash. It makes you irritable, slow, and useless. She will notice. You will notice. Prevent it.

### THE TIMELINE

**EARLY LABOR (Hours 0–6): EAT A REAL MEAL.**
- This is your window. She's at home or in triage. Contractions are manageable. You have time to sit, chew, and digest.
- Eat something with **protein, fat, and complex carbs.** A turkey sandwich. Eggs and toast. Pasta with chicken. Rice and beans. Oatmeal with peanut butter and a banana.
- *Do not* skip this meal because you're "too nervous." You will regret it at hour 12.
- Drink a full glass of water. Have a coffee if you want one. This is the time.

**ACTIVE LABOR (Hours 6–12): GRAZE. SMALL. FREQUENT. ONE-HANDED.**
- You will not want a full meal. Your stomach will be in knots. That's fine. You don't need a meal. You need *fuel.*
- **Eat something every 90 minutes to 2 hours.** Set a timer on your phone if you have to. "Eat now." You don't have to want it. Eat it.
- Best options:
  - Beef or turkey jerky (protein, salt, one-handed)
  - A banana (potassium, easy on the stomach, no prep)
  - A handful of almonds or trail mix (fat + protein, slow burn)
  - Peanut butter squeeze packets (calorie-dense, no mess)
  - Cheese sticks
  - Crackers with peanut butter
  - A granola bar (the kind with actual oats and nuts, not a candy bar in a wrapper)
  - Pretzels or saltines (easy on a nervous stomach, salt helps with electrolytes)
- **Avoid:** Greasy fast food (you'll feel sick), heavy dairy if your stomach is nervous, anything with a strong smell if she's nausea-prone (she will notice your tuna sandwich from across the room and it will not go well).

**TRANSITION & PUSHING (Hours 12–18+): QUICK SUGAR + PROTEIN. SURVIVAL MODE.**
- You are running on fumes. You need fast energy that won't sit in your stomach.
  - A few sips of sports drink or juice (fast sugar, electrolytes)
  - A banana or a few crackers
  - A peanut butter packet
  - A handful of gummy bears or a few squares of dark chocolate (quick glucose, a small morale boost)
- **Do not** try to eat a full sandwich while she's pushing. You don't have time and your hands are on her hips.
- **Do** take three bites of something every time there's a break between contractions. Three bites. That's it. Keep the engine running.

**AFTER THE BIRTH: EAT SOMETHING REAL.**
- The adrenaline will dump. You may shake. You may cry. You may feel suddenly, violently hungry or slightly nauseous. All normal.
- Eat the sandwich you packed. Drink a full glass of water. Sit down. You earned it.

### HYDRATION — THE NON-NEGOTIABLE RULE
- **Drink water every time she has a contraction.** You're standing there anyway. Sip. You'll get 30–40 oz in over a few hours without thinking about it.
- **Alternate water with an electrolyte drink.** Plain water for 12 hours will flush your sodium and you'll get a headache and feel foggy. One sports drink or electrolyte packet every 3–4 hours.
- **Limit coffee to one or two cups, early.** (See Part 3.)
- **Urine check:** If you haven't peed in 4+ hours, you're dehydrated. Drink. If your urine is dark yellow, drink more.

---

## PART 3: CAFFEINE — A STRATEGY, NOT A HABIT

Caffeine is a tool. Used well, it keeps you sharp. Used badly, it makes you jittery, anxious, and crashes you at the worst moment.

- **Early labor (hours 0–6):** Have your coffee. A normal cup. Maybe two. You're still in the "waiting" phase and you want to be alert.
- **Active labor (hours 6–12):** Switch to a *small* amount. Half a cup of coffee. A few sips of an energy drink. You want a gentle lift, not a spike.
- **Transition/pushing (hours 12+):** **Be careful.** If you haven't eaten, caffeine on an empty stomach will make you shaky, nauseous, and anxious. If you need a boost, have a *small* amount with food. A few sips of coffee with a banana. Not a 16-oz cold brew on an empty stomach at hour 14.
- **The crash rule:** Caffeine peaks at 30–60 minutes and wears off at 4–6 hours. If you drink a large coffee at hour 2, you will crash at hour 7. Time it. A small coffee at hour 8 carries you through hour 13.
- **Do not** use caffeine as a substitute for food or a 20-minute nap. It is not. It is a supplement to both.

## PART 4: ENERGY MANAGEMENT — THE 20-HOUR PACING PLAN

This is the section that will matter most. Read it twice.

### THE MISTAKE EVERYONE MAKES
You run on pure adrenaline for 10 hours. You don't sit. You don't eat. You don't leave the room. You white-knuckle every contraction. And then, at hour 13, when she hits transition and needs you *most* — when she's vomiting, shaking, screaming "I can't do this" — **you are empty.** Your hands are shaking. Your back is locked. Your brain is fog. You can't do the hip squeezes. You can't make eye contact. You can't remember the phrases.

You cannot sprint a marathon. You have to pace.

### THE RULES

**Rule 1: Sit down whenever you can.**
- During early labor: sit in the chair. Put your feet up. Lean back. You don't need to stand at attention while she's scrolling Instagram between 4-minute contractions.
- During active labor: sit on the edge of the bed. Sit on a birth ball. Lean against the wall. Do hip squeezes from a *stable* position, not while balancing on one foot.
- During the breaks between contractions: *sit.* Even for 30 seconds. Take the load off your legs and back.
- **Your lower back will be your undoing.** You'll be hunched over the bed, pressing on her sacrum, for hours. Counteract it: stand up straight between contractions. Roll your shoulders. Put your hands on your lower back and lean backward for 10 seconds. Do this every 20 minutes.

**Rule 2: Tag out. Use your people.**
- If there is a doula, a mother, a sister, a friend in the room or in the hallway: **use them.** You are not failing if you step out for 20 minutes.
- Say: *"I'm going to grab food and use the bathroom. I'll be back in 20 minutes. Can you sit with her?"*
- Go to the family room. Sit down. Eat a sandwich. Close your eyes for 10 minutes. Brush your teeth. Change your shirt. Come back.
- **You are not abandoning her.** You are refueling so you can be *present* for the next 6 hours. A 20-minute break at hour 8 buys you clarity at hour 14.
- If she's in early labor and resting, this is the time. If she's in transition, you don't leave. Know the difference.

**Rule 3: Manage your own nervous system.**
- You will get an adrenaline surge when she screams, when there's blood, when the nurse's tone changes. Your heart will pound. Your hands will shake. Your breathing will go shallow.
- **Box breathing.** In for 4. Hold for 4. Out for 4. Hold for 4. Do it four times. Do it in the hallway. Do it while you're washing your hands. Do it while you're pretending to check your phone.
- **Name what you're feeling.** "I'm scared. That's okay. I'm scared and I'm still here." You don't have to perform fearlessness. You have to perform *steadiness.* Those are different.
- **One contraction at a time.** Don't think about the next 8 hours. Think about the next 90 seconds. "This contraction will end. Then the next one will come. And it will end too." This is what you say to her. Say it to yourself, too.

**Rule 4: Protect the sleep window.**
- If labor starts at night (it often does), and she's in early labor — contractions 8–10 minutes apart, she's dozing — **you rest too.** Lie down on the couch. Set an alarm for 30 minutes. Close your eyes. Even if you don't sleep, the rest matters.
- At the hospital, if she's resting between contractions in early/active labor and a support person is in the room: put your head down on the bed, on a pillow, on your arms. 10 minutes of eyes-closed rest is worth more than you know.
- **You will not sleep much.** Accept that. You're not trying to get 8 hours. You're trying to get three 20-minute windows of rest over 16 hours. That's the realistic goal.

**Rule 5: Move your body.**
- You will be stiff. Your hips, your back, your shoulders, your neck. Every hour, do this:
  - Roll your shoulders backward 10 times.
  - Stand up straight, hands on your lower back, lean back gently. Hold 10 seconds.
  - Roll your neck side to side.
  - Shake out your hands and wrists. (Counter-pressure cramps the hands. Open and close your fists 10 times.)
  - Walk to the water fountain and back. Even if it's 30 steps. Move.
- **Your hands will cramp from hip squeezes and counter-pressure.** This is real. Alternate hands. Use your forearms instead of your thumbs. Use a tennis ball or a rolled-up washcloth as a pressure tool so your hands get a break. Ask the nurse for a "peanut ball" or a "rebozo" — tools that do some of the physical work for you.

**Rule 6: Know the emotional arc so it doesn't blindside you.**

| Hours | You'll feel | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 0–4 | Nervous excitement. "This is happening!" Adrenaline. | Eat the real meal. Pack the last items. Breathe. Don't over-perform. |
| 4–8 | Boredom. Anxiety. "Is this going to take forever?" | Read. Walk. Snack. Tag out if you can. Don't hover. |
| 8–12 | Helplessness. "I can't fix this." Fatigue sets in. | Focus on the physical jobs. Hip squeezes. Cool cloth. You don't need to fix. You need to *do.* |
| 12–16 | Fear. "Something must be wrong." Exhaustion. | This is the danger zone for you. Eat. Drink. Breathe. Remind yourself: *this is the hardest part and it's the shortest.* |
| 16–20 | Numbness. Surrender. "Whatever happens, I'm here." | You're in the final stretch. Lean on the nurse. Lean on the doula. You just need to be *present.* |
| Birth | Elation. Tears. Shaking. "Holy hell." | Cry if you need to. You earned it. Hold the baby. Hold her. Drink water. |
| 1–2 hrs after | The adrenaline dump. Shaking, crying, sudden exhaustion, or a strange flatness. | **This is normal.** You are not "not happy enough." Your body is coming down from 20 hours of fight-or-flight. Eat. Sit. Hold the baby. The feeling will come. |

---

## PART 5: THE 3 A.M. HALLWAY MOMENT

It's 3 a.m. You've been here for 9 hours. She's at 6 cm. The nurse just did another cervical check. You're standing in the hallway under fluorescent lights. Your back aches. Your phone is at 14%. You had a banana four hours ago. You feel like you're losing your mind a little.

**This is the moment.** This is where most partners quietly fall apart. Not in the room. In the hallway. Alone. Under the bad lighting.

Here's what you do:

1. **Lean against the wall.** Physically. Take the weight off your feet.
2. **Drink water.** The whole bottle. Right now.
3. **Eat something.** The jerky. The peanut butter packet. The granola bar. Unwrap it. Eat it. All of it.
4. **Breathe.** Four in. Four hold. Four out. Four hold. Five times.
5. **Text someone.** Your mom. Your best friend. Your brother. "It's been 9 hours. She's at 6. I'm tired. I'm okay. Just needed to say it to a human." You don't need advice. You need to say it out loud (or out-finger) to someone who isn't in the room.
6. **Go to the bathroom.** Wash your hands. Wash your face. Look in the mirror. Say, out loud, quietly: *"You're doing fine. She's doing fine. You're halfway through. Go back in there."*
7. **Go back in.** Sit down. Put your hand on her back. Breathe with her.

You don't have to be a hero. You have to be a person who eats a granola bar in the hallway and goes back in.

That's the whole job.

---

## THE ONE-PAGE SUMMARY — SCREENSHOT THIS

**PACK:** Snacks (jerky, nuts, banana, PB packets, crackers). Real meal. Water bottle. Electrolytes. Coffee (small). Gum. Change of clothes. Sneakers. Hoodie. Phone charger + battery. Cash. Earbuds. Pen + notebook. Toothbrush. Deodorant. Birth plan × 3.

**EAT:** Real meal in early labor. Snack every 90 min in active labor. Quick sugar + protein in transition/pushing. Real meal after birth. **Eat before you're hungry.**

**DRINK:** Water every contraction. Electrolyte drink every 3–4 hrs. Limit coffee to 1–2 cups, early. **If your urine is dark, drink more.**

**REST:** Sit whenever you can. Tag out for 20 min during early/active labor. Nap when she naps. 10 minutes of eyes-closed rest counts.

**MOVE:** Shoulders, back, neck, wrists. Every hour. Shake out your hands. Walk to the water fountain.

**BREATHE:** Box breathing when the adrenaline hits. 4-4-4-4. Five rounds. Hallway. Bathroom. Anywhere.

**THE RULE:** You are an endurance athlete. Pace yourself. You cannot pour from an empty cup. A fed, rested, hydrated partner at hour 14 is worth ten unfed, running-on-adrenaline partners at hour 6.

---

Now go make that sandwich. Pack the bag. Fill the water bottle. Set your alarm for 30 minutes and lie down.

She's got the hardest job in the room. You've got the second-hardest. And you can do the second-hardest for 20 hours if you eat the damn granola bar.

You've got this.

— *Penny*

# THE 60-SECOND SCRIPT: TRANSITION, SCREAMING, "I WANT AN EPIDURAL"
### *Read this now. Read it again in the hallway. When it happens, you will know what to do.*

---

She's 8 cm. Maybe 9. Contractions are 90 seconds long, 2 minutes apart. She's been doing this for an hour. She's shaking. She vomited twenty minutes ago. And now a contraction hits and she grabs the bed rail, her whole body locks, and she screams — not moans, *screams* — **"I CAN'T DO THIS. I CAN'T. I WANT AN EPIDURAL. MAKE IT STOP. I CAN'T DO THIS ANYMORE."**

You freeze. Your brain says *do something, fix it, call someone, agree, disagree, SAY SOMETHING.*

Here's what you do. Second by second. I'm going to walk you through the next 60 seconds like I'm standing behind you with my hand on your shoulder.

**Go.**

---

## SECONDS 0–3: DON'T TALK YET. MOVE.

She's mid-scream. Mid-contraction. She is not processing language right now. Her nervous system is in full alarm. Words will not land yet.

**Your body moves before your mouth does.**

- **Step in.** Close the distance. You are now 12 inches from her face. Not across the bed. Not at the foot. *Beside her.* At her shoulder. In her peripheral vision.
- **Get low.** If she's on the bed, bend your knees. If she's on hands-and-knees, kneel beside her. Your face should be at *her* level or slightly below. Do not tower over her. Do not stand at the foot of the bed looking down. Get in her eyeline.
- **Put your hands on her.** Not a gentle pat. Not a tentative touch. **Firm. Grounding. Both hands.** On her shoulders. On her hips. On her face — cup her jaw gently with both hands and turn her face toward yours. She needs to feel that you are *here*, solid, real, not going anywhere. The pressure of your hands is an anchor.

**Do not say "shh." Do not say "it's okay." Do not say "calm down." Do not say "you're doing great."** None of those land. None of those are true in this moment. She is not okay. She is not calm. She is not "great." She is in the hardest 90 seconds of her life.

Say nothing yet. Move. Touch. Get in her sightline.

---

## SECONDS 3–10: MAKE EYE CONTACT. BREATHE FIRST.

She's still screaming. Her eyes may be shut. She may be looking through you, not at you.

- **Say her name.** Not "honey." Not "baby." Her *name.* The one you use when you need her to hear you. Firm. Low. Not shouted.
  > **"[Name]. [Name]. Look at me. Open your eyes. Look at me."**

- **Wait for her eyes.** They'll find yours. Maybe for two seconds. Maybe she'll squeeze them shut again. That's fine. You got them.

- **Now you breathe. Exaggerated. Visible. Audible.** She will not be able to find her own breath right now. She's panting, gasping, holding it, losing it. You become her metronome.
  - Breathe in through your nose, slow, 4 counts. *Loud enough for her to hear.*
  - Breathe out through your mouth, slow, 6 counts. A soft "fffff" or "hoooo."
  - Do it again. And again. **You are breathing FOR her until she can breathe WITH you.**
  - Say it:
    > **"Breathe with me. In... [breathe in, loud]... and out... [breathe out, long]... In... and out... That's it. With me. In... and out."**

- Your voice is **low, slow, steady.** Not high. Not fast. Not panicked. You are the calmest sound in the room. The nurse is checking the monitor. The bed is creaking. She is screaming. Your voice is the one slow, low, steady thing. *Be that thing.*

---

## SECONDS 10–45: THE CONTRACTION. YOUR HANDS AND YOUR WORDS.

The contraction is still going. It will peak around second 30–40 and then start to fade. This is the longest 35 seconds of your life. Here's what you do with your hands and your mouth simultaneously.

### YOUR HANDS (pick one, do it firmly):
- **Double hip squeeze:** Both hands on the bony points of her hips. Squeeze *inward and up.* Hard. She will not say "that's too hard." She will lean into it.
- **Sacral counter-pressure:** Heel of your hand on the flat bone at the base of her spine. Press *hard* and steady. Lean your body weight into it. Don't push with your thumb — you'll cramp. Use the heel of your palm or a tennis ball.
- **If she's gripping the rail and won't let go:** One hand on her sacrum, one hand over her gripping hand. Squeeze. She needs to feel your hand *on* hers. Solid.

### YOUR WORDS (low, slow, rhythmic, timed to her breathing):
You are not giving a speech. You are repeating short, true phrases. Like a mantra. Like a coach. Say them on the exhale.

> **"You ARE doing it."**
> *(In... out.)*
> **"You are right in the middle of it."**
> *(In... out.)*
> **"This contraction will end."**
> *(In... out.)*
> **"I'm right here. I'm not going anywhere."**
> *(In... out.)*
> **"You are doing it. You are doing it. You are doing it."**

**Do NOT say:**
- ❌ "You're doing great." *(She doesn't feel great. This feels dismissive.)*
- ❌ "Almost there!" *(You don't know. And "almost" can mean 20 more minutes. She'll hate you.)*
- ❌ "It's almost over." *(Same problem.)*
- ❌ "You wanted a natural birth, remember?" *(DO NOT. SAY. THIS. See below.)*
- ❌ "Calm down." *(Never. In any context. In any labor. Never.)*
- ❌ "I know, I know, I know" said fast and high. *(This sounds like YOU are panicking. Slow it down. "I know" said low and slow is validating. Said fast and high, it's your anxiety leaking out.)*

### IF SHE SAYS "GET AWAY FROM ME" OR PUSHES YOU:
- **Don't take it personally.** She is not rejecting you. She is in a storm and you are the nearest solid object. She may shove your hand. She may say "don't touch me." She may say "I hate you."
- **Pull back six inches. Do not leave the room.** Remove your hands. Stay in her sightline. Say:
  > **"Okay. I'm right here. I'm not touching you. I'm right here."**
- **Wait 10 seconds.** She will likely reach for you again. Or the next contraction will hit and she'll grab your arm. Be there. No ego. No hurt face. You are a tool she's using. Sometimes the tool is "hands on hips." Sometimes the tool is "a voice in the room." Sometimes the tool is "a person she can scream at." All of it is the job.

---

## SECONDS 45–55: THE CONTRACTION FADES. SHE SLUMPS.

The peak passes. Her body unclenches. She slumps forward, or back, or sideways. She's gasping. Maybe crying. Maybe shaking. The scream is over. She's in the *aftermath* of the contraction — the 60–120 second window before the next one builds.

**This is when you talk. Not during the scream. Now.**

- **Keep your hands on her.** One hand on her back. One on her shoulder. Or wipe her forehead with the cool cloth. Or hold a sip of water to her lips. *Do something small and physical.*
- **Say, low and steady:**
  > **"That one's over. You did it. That one is done. Breathe. Just breathe. I've got you."**

- **Wipe her face.** The cool washcloth. Forehead. Cheeks. She's drenched in sweat. This small act says *I see you, I'm taking care of you, you are not alone in this mess.*

- **Get the water.** Hold the straw to her lips. "Sip. Just a sip." She may push it away. That's fine. Try again next contraction.

---

## SECONDS 55–60: THE EPIDURAL QUESTION. THE REAL ONE.

She said "I want an epidural." You heard her. You did not ignore it. You did not argue. You got her through the contraction. **Now, in the break, you address it.**

Lean in. Eye level. Low voice. Just for her. Not for the nurse. Not for the room.

> **"Hey. Look at me. You said you want an epidural. I heard you. I want to ask you something, and you don't have to answer right now. Do you want me to call the nurse and ask about it? Or do you want me to keep doing what I'm doing and we get through the next one together? Either answer is okay. I will do whatever you say."**

**Then you wait. You look at her. You let her answer.**

### WHAT HER ANSWER MEANS:

**If she says "I want the epidural. Get the nurse. I can't do this anymore."**
- You say: **"Okay. I'm going to get the nurse right now. I'll be back in thirty seconds. [Name/doula] is here with you."**
- You walk to the door. You flag the nurse. You say: **"She's requesting pain relief. She'd like to talk to someone about an epidural. Can you come in?"**
- You go back. You get back in her sightline. You say: **"The nurse is coming. I'm right here. You made a decision and that's okay. There is no wrong answer. I'm proud of you."**
- **You do NOT say "but you wanted—" You do NOT say "are you sure?" You do NOT sigh. You do NOT look disappointed.** She is allowed to change her mind. She is allowed to be in too much pain. She is allowed to want relief. Your job is to get the nurse, not to enforce a birth plan. *Her body. Her call. Full stop.* (See Mistake #3 in the field guide. This is the test. Pass it.)

**If she says "I don't know" or "I can't think" or just cries and shakes her head:**
- You say: **"You don't have to decide right now. Let's get through the next contraction. I'll ask you again after. I'm right here."**
- You buy her one more contraction. And another. Transition is *short.* You may be 20 minutes from pushing. You don't need to decide this second. You just need to get through the next 90 seconds. And the next.

**If she says "NO. No epidural. I just — I can't — I just need you to —" and she can't finish the sentence:**
- You say: **"I know. I know. You don't want the epidural. You just need me to help you through this. I've got you. Hands on your hips. Breathe with me. We're doing this. Next one's coming and we'll do it together. I'm right here."**
- You get your hands back on her hips. You get your breathing going. You anchor. **This is what "I can't do this" usually means in transition.** It means *"I am doing this and it is more than I have ever felt and I need you to tell me I'm not losing my mind."* You tell her. You tell her every single time.

**If she says "I want an epidural" but the nurse says "you're too far along / it's too late / the anesthesiologist is 20 minutes out":**
- You say to the nurse: **"What CAN we do right now for pain? Nitrous oxide? IV pain medication? A different position? What are the options?"**
- You go back to her. You say: **"The epidural would take [X] minutes to set up. The nurse says [option] is available right now. Do you want to try that? Or do you want me to keep doing the hip squeezes and we push through? I'm with you either way."**
- You do NOT say "well, I guess you can't have one." You say: **"Here's what's available. What do you want?"**

## THE NEXT 2 MINUTES: THE WINDOW BETWEEN CONTRACTIONS

The next contraction is building. You have 90–120 seconds. Here's your checklist:

- [ ] **Cool cloth.** Forehead. Back of neck. Replace it.
- [ ] **Water.** Straw to her lips. "Sip."
- [ ] **Wipe her face.** She's soaked.
- [ ] **Check her position.** Is she tense in a way that's making it harder? "Can you drop your shoulders? Unclench your jaw? Let your hands go loose?" (Tension in the jaw and shoulders tightens the pelvic floor. Relaxing the jaw can actually help the cervix. This is a real, physiological thing. Tell her: *"Loosen your jaw. Let your mouth go soft. It helps."*)
- [ ] **Your body.** Shake out your hands. Roll your shoulders. Take a breath. You just did 90 seconds of maximum-intensity support. You need 30 seconds to reset.
- [ ] **The room.** Is the light too bright? Dim it. Is someone talking? "Quiet, please." Is the door open? Close it. Protect the space.
- [ ] **You.** Box breath. 4-4-4-4. Two rounds. You are allowed to be scared. You are allowed to be exhausted. You are not allowed to show her that you're falling apart. Save it for the hallway. Right now, you are the steadiest thing in the room.

And then the next contraction starts. And you do it again.

And again.

And again.

And it's shorter than you think. **Transition is the shortest stage.** When it feels the absolute worst, it is the closest to being over. You are closer to the end than the beginning. Hold that.

## THE ONE THING TO REMEMBER WHEN YOU'RE STANDING THERE AND YOUR BRAIN GOES BLANK

You will forget the scripts. You will forget the breathing counts. You will forget the phrases. Your mind will go white and you'll think *what do I say, what do I say, what do I—*

Here's the one thing. The only thing you actually need. Everything else is bonus.

**Get in her face. Put your hands on her. Breathe slow and loud. And say:**

> **"You are doing it. I am here. This will end. You are not alone."**

That's it. That's the whole thing. Four sentences. Say them on every exhale. Say them for 90 seconds. Say them again when the next one comes.

You don't need to be eloquent. You don't need to be calm. You need to be *there*, with your hands on her and those four words in your mouth.

She will remember this moment for the rest of her life. Not the words. Not the technique. The fact that when she was screaming, when she was lost, when she was sure she was breaking — **you got in her face, you put your hands on her, and you did not look away.**

You stayed. You breathed. You said *you are doing it.*

And she was.
