# The Birth Partner's Field Guide

### *Read this in the parking lot. Read it again in the hallway. You'll be fine. She'll be fine. Here's what's actually coming.*

You've never seen a birth. You're nervous. Good — nervous means you care, and caring is 80% of this job. This is the other 20%. It's written to be re-read in five minutes on the day, so it's organized like a field guide, not an essay: **what you'll see → your jobs → the mistakes → the phrases → the red flags → the scripts.**

---

## PART 1: WHAT YOU WILL ACTUALLY SEE, HEAR, AND FEEL

Forget the movies. Here's the real thing, stage by stage.

### EARLY LABOR (0–6 cm) — *The Long Warm-Up*

- **What you see:** A woman who is mostly herself. She'll be restless — pacing, swaying, leaning on furniture, stopping mid-sentence to breathe. Contractions are 5–10 minutes apart and last 30–60 seconds. She can talk between them.
- **What you hear:** Conversation, sighs, maybe some low moans as things pick up. She may be chatty, or she may go quiet and inward. Both are normal.
- **What she looks like during a contraction:** She stops moving, closes her eyes or stares at a point, and breathes. Her face might scrunch slightly. Then she comes back.
- **Your job right now:** Stay chill. Do NOT time every contraction obsessively. Do NOT announce "here comes another one!" Do NOT rush to the hospital. Feed her, hydrate her, distract her, let her rest. This phase is usually the longest part of labor — often many hours — and exhausting yourself in it is the classic rookie mistake.

### ACTIVE LABOR (6–8 cm) — *The Shift*

- **What you see:** She stops being social. She goes inward. She'll find her position — hands-and-knees on the bed, leaning over the birth ball, standing in the shower — and she'll *work*.
- **What you hear:** Deep, low moans. Guttural sounds. Not screaming — low. Think "fogging a mirror" sounds. She may not want to talk at all. She may not want to be touched in certain moments. She'll tell you.
- **What she looks like during a contraction:** Her whole body tenses and then releases with the wave. Her face may look intense, focused, even pained — but between contractions she melts, often into you or the bed.
- **Your job:** Physical support (counter-pressure, hip squeeze), hydration, position changes every 30 minutes or so, and matching her breathing. Keep your voice low and slow. Follow her lead.

### TRANSITION (8–10 cm) — *The Storm. The Shortest, Hardest Part.*

- **What you see:** This is the most intense part — and usually the shortest (minutes to a couple of hours). She may shake, vomit, sweat, cry, or get angry. She may thrash or collapse between waves. She may say *"I can't do this."*
- **What you hear:** Loud, primal, low-pitched sounds — roaring, groaning, sometimes "I can't," "help me," "make it stop." High-pitched screaming is a signal to help her lower her voice and breath, not panic.
- **What she looks like:** Exhausted and enormous at the same time. Her eyes may be closed the whole phase. She may push you away and then grab you.
- **Your job:** Do not take it personally. Do not argue. Do not try to "fix" it. Get in close, match her breathing, apply pressure where she wants it, and say the calm, short things from the script in Part 6. This phase means the baby is nearly here — treat it as good news, even if it looks like chaos.

### PUSHING (Second Stage) — *The Work*

- **What you see:** The urge to push takes over. Her body will bear down with each wave, often with a long, low grunt or groan. Her face goes red. She may make sounds you've never heard from a human.
- **What you hear:** Deep, sustained pushing sounds — like someone lifting something very heavy. Not screaming — *effort*.
- **What she looks like:** Utterly focused, animal, powerful. Between pushes she may be completely limp.
- **Your job:** Encouragement in short, concrete phrases ("that was a good one," "you're so close," "your body is doing this"). Count only if she or the staff ask you to. Hold her hand, support her back, hold the cool cloth. This can last a few minutes to a few hours for a first baby. Do not check "how much longer" — that's not a thing anyone can answer usefully.

### THIRD STAGE (Placenta) — *The Afterthought*

- **What you see:** Baby is out and on her chest. Everyone is crying. Then, minutes later, she has a few more mild contractions to deliver the placenta. It looks like a liver-ish disc, and it's totally normal.
- **Your job:** Stay out of the way. Hold her hand, tell her she did it, and be ready to be the one who remembers the camera or the text to the family. This is the moment everyone worked for.

## PART 2: YOUR TOP 5 JOBS, RANKED

### **#1: BE THE CALM ONE.**

You are the thermostat of the room. When you're calm, the room is calm. When you panic, everyone feels it. Your #1 job is not to do anything impressive — it's to *not add chaos*. Breathe slowly. Speak low. Move slowly. She will mirror you.

### **#2: BE THE PHYSICAL COMFORT ENGINE.**

Your hands matter more than your words. Learn these now:
- **Counter-pressure:** Press firmly with the heel of your hand into her lower back (sacrum) during a contraction. She will tell you "harder" — believe her; it's hard to press too hard for back labor.
- **Double hip squeeze:** Place one hand on each hip and squeeze firmly inward during a contraction.
- **The cool cloth / warm compress:** Cool on her forehead and neck; warm on her lower back. Ask which she wants.
- **Position help:** When she's stuck, offer one specific option: *"Stand up for this one and lean on me?"* or *"Let's try hands-and-knees for the next one."* One suggestion at a time, never a lecture.

### **#3: BE THE TIMEKEEPER AND PATTERN-READER.**

You track what she can't: when contractions started, how long they last, how far apart, whether her water broke, and what the nurse needs to know. Write it down — you will forget. Note: during early labor at home, check the pattern occasionally, not every contraction.

### **#4: BE THE GATEKEEPER.**

Protect her space. That means: fewer people in the room, not more. Quiet voices. Lights low. Phones away. You are the one who says, gently, *"She needs quiet right now — can we talk in the hall?"* She shouldn't have to ask; that's your job.

### **#5: BE THE ADVOCATE.**

You are her second voice when she can't use hers. This is a skill with exact phrases — see Part 4. Advocacy is not arguing with staff; it's asking questions, buying time, and making sure her wishes are actually heard.

## PART 3: THE 3 BIGGEST MISTAKES PARTNERS MAKE

### **Mistake #1: Trying to fix her pain.**

*"Have you tried breathing?" "Just relax." "Maybe you should take the epidural."* — All useless. You cannot fix labor. You can only *be with* her in it.

**Do instead:** Be quiet and useful. Hands on, breath matched, mouth shut. The best sentence in labor is often *"I'm right here."*

### **Mistake #2: Disappearing or freezing when it gets intense.**

Transition is overwhelming for you too — but if you check out, she's alone. Partners who freeze usually do it because they're scared, and that's human. The fix is to have a physical job: press here, hold this, breathe with me.

**Do instead:** Give yourself one simple task per contraction: counter-pressure, the hip squeeze, the cool cloth, the breathing. Movement breaks the freeze.

### **Mistake #3: Taking her words personally or negotiating with her.**

*"You're being mean." "Calm down." "You said you wanted natural — are you sure?"* — In transition, she may say things she doesn't mean. It's not about you. Arguing in labor is like arguing with a hurricane.

**Do instead:** Treat everything she says as information, not an attack. If she says "get away from me," back off one step but stay in the room: *"Okay, I'm here when you need me."* If she says "I want an epidural," don't debate — use the script in Part 6.

## PART 4: ADVOCATING FOR HER — THE EXACT PHRASES

Advocacy is not confrontation. It's asking questions calmly until you understand, and using time as a tool. These phrases work with nurses and doctors — they're respectful, and they buy space:

**To understand the recommendation:**
- *"Can you help us understand the reason for this recommendation right now?"*
- *"What are we hoping this achieves?"*
- *"What happens if we wait another hour?"*
- *"Is this urgent, or do we have time to talk it through?"*

**To buy time:**
- *"We'd like a few minutes to discuss this together — can we pause?"*
- *"Could we try [specific alternative] for the next hour and reassess?"*
- *"She's asking to wait. Can we check again in an hour before deciding?"*

**To push back when you feel her wishes are being overridden:**
- *"I understand what you're recommending. We're not declining — we'd like more time and more information before agreeing."*
- *"Can you write down the risks you're concerned about, so we can make an informed decision?"*
- *"This is her birth. We'd appreciate being consulted before any procedure, unless it's a true emergency."*
- *"If there's a reason this can't wait, please tell us what it is — we'll say yes if it's urgent."*

**The golden rule:** Never say yes or no on her behalf without checking with her first. Turn to her, repeat what you heard, and ask: *"They're suggesting [X] because [Y]. What do you think?"* Then say what she decides. If she can't speak, you speak — but you speak *her* wishes, not your own.

## PART 5: NORMAL PAIN vs. SOMETHING IS WRONG

You can't see what's happening inside, so use these external signs.

### **THIS IS NORMAL — DO NOT PANIC:**

- Intense pain that comes in waves and completely eases between them
- Moaning, groaning, roaring, crying, swearing — any low sound
- Vomiting, shaking, sweating, hot flashes, chills
- Saying "I can't do this," "I want to go home," "make it stop"
- Pushing you away, then grabbing you — she's overwhelmed, not rejecting you
- A gush or trickle of clear or pink-tinged fluid (waters breaking)
- A show of blood-streaked mucus (bloody show)

### **SOMETHING MAY BE WRONG — GET A NURSE OR PROVIDER NOW:**

- **Bright red bleeding** (more than a show — like a period, or gushing)
- **Green or foul-smelling fluid** when her waters break (could be meconium)
- **Cord visible** at the vaginal opening — do not touch it, get help immediately
- **She can't speak or is confused/disoriented** between contractions
- **She has a fever** (especially with chills that don't stop)
- **Severe headache, vision changes, or pain in her upper right belly** — these can signal preeclampsia
- **Baby's movements have dropped** or stopped (this is checked before labor too)
- Contractions that last longer than 90 seconds or never let up in between — that's not "intense," that's a pattern worth flagging

### **The Simple Rule:**

If you're wondering whether something is wrong — **call the nurse.** That's what they're for. You will never be penalized for asking. "I'd rather check" is a completely valid job.

## PART 6: THE 60-SECOND SCRIPT — TRANSITION, SCREAMING, "I WANT AN EPIDURAL"

It's 2 a.m. She's at 9 cm, shaking, sobbing, and she screams: **"I can't do this. I want an epidural."**

Here's what you do, second by second.

**Seconds 0–3: Don't talk. Move.**
Get close. Take her hand or put your hand on her back. Match her breathing with one big audible breath — in through your nose, long slow out through your mouth. Show her the rhythm with your own body.

**Seconds 3–10: One sentence, said slowly, twice.**
*"You're almost there. You can do anything for one more contraction."* Then, quieter: *"You're so close. We're right here."*

Do not say: *"But you wanted natural!"* Do not argue. Do not explain. Do not say *"are you sure?"* in a disappointed tone.

**Seconds 10–45: Work the contraction with her.**
- Get your hands busy: counter-pressure on the lower back, or both hands squeezing her hips, or your forehead against hers.
- Breathe with her, audibly, low and slow. Say one thing on repeat, timed to her exhale: *"That's it. Out. Let it go. Good."*
- If she's high and tight, invite her lower: *"Drop your jaw. Make it low. Down to your baby."* Moan with her if that helps — matching her sound steers her from panic to power.
- Do not leave, do not stop, do not look at the clock.

**Seconds 45–55: The wave fades.**
*"Good. That one's done. You did that one."* Let her slump. Wipe her face. Water if she wants it. Rest in the silence.

**Seconds 55–60: Answer the epidural question — honestly, without drama.**
This is the part people get wrong. You don't have to talk her out of it. You say:

*"If you still want the epidural after this next contraction, I'll get the nurse. You decide. But I want you to know — the fact that you're saying 'I can't' right now usually means you're almost done. Let's do one more, and then you choose."*

**Why this works:** It gives her control (the thing she's actually losing), it doesn't shame her, and it's honest — if she truly wants it, you'll get it. And in the vast majority of cases, by the time the next contraction passes, the urge to push has arrived and the question answers itself.

**The next 2 minutes:** If she still wants the epidural, get the nurse — no guilt, no lectures. If the staff checks and she's fully dilated, the epidural ship has often sailed anyway, and everyone pivots to pushing. Either way, you did it right: you stayed calm, you kept her in control, and you never fought her.

## PART 7: THE PITOCIN CONVERSATION — A ROLE-PLAY SCRIPT

**The scene:** The nurse says her labor has slowed and suggests Pitocin to speed things up. She wants to wait. You're the one who has to navigate it.

**What you say, in order:**

1. **Get the information first — don't agree or decline yet.**
   > *"Thanks for letting us know. Can you help us understand why it's being suggested right now? Is it a concern for her or the baby, or is it about the pace of labor?"*

2. **If the answer is "just to speed things up":**
   > *"We understand. She'd like to try more time — can we do another hour of walking, position changes, and rest, and then reassess?"*

3. **If the nurse pushes back:**
   > *"We're not refusing — we'd just like to try the least invasive option first. What would we be watching for in the next hour that would change your recommendation?"*

4. **Turn to her — always.**
   > *"They're suggesting Pitocin to speed things up. There's no immediate concern right now, but they'd like to move faster. What do you think?"* — and then say exactly what she says.

5. **If she wants to wait:**
   > *"She'd like to wait another hour and try moving first. We'll check back in at [time]. Is that okay?"*

6. **If the nurse says it's medically necessary (e.g., baby's heart rate or infection risk):**
   > *"Okay — if that's the concern, then let's do it. Can you walk us through what happens next?"* — Say yes to real medicine. That's not failure; that's the job.

**What NOT to say:** *"She really wants natural"* (makes her sound naive), *"Can't you just leave her alone?"* (makes you an adversary), *"I'll ask her"* without actually asking her.

**The cheat sheet:** Ask why → ask what happens if you wait → ask what you're watching for → check with her → say what she decided. You never have to say "no" to staff. You just need to understand, wait, and choose.

## PART 8: TAKING CARE OF YOURSELF — YOU'RE A MARATHON RUNNER NOW

Labor can last 12–20+ hours. You are no use to her if you're dehydrated, starving, and vibrating with caffeine at hour 14. Plan for yourself like it's a race.

### Pack for yourself (separate from her bag):
- **Food:** Dense, quiet, non-crunchy snacks — granola bars, trail mix, peanut-butter sandwiches, dried fruit, crackers. NO loud bags (you'll be the most-hated person in the room).
- **Drinks:** 2+ bottles of water, a sports drink, and one coffee/tea for the overnight shift. Also mints or gum.
- **Clothes:** Layers — hospital rooms run hot and cold. Comfortable shoes (you'll stand for hours). A hoodie. Sweatpants or athletic pants.
- **Gear:** Phone charger + long cable, power bank, a small notebook and pen (for timing and notes), a pillow if you think you'll nap in the chair, toiletries, and a change of clothes.
- **Distraction for long waits:** A book, downloaded shows, earbuds — for *between* the action, not during it.

### Eat and drink on a schedule:
- Eat a real meal **before** you leave the house.
- Set a phone alarm every 2–3 hours: eat a snack, drink water, use the bathroom. Even if she doesn't want anything, you do it.
- Keep your energy steady: small snacks through the whole labor, not one big meal at hour 8. Avoid anything greasy or heavy.
- **Hydration rule:** one glass of water every 1–2 hours, minimum. Dehydration makes you shaky, foggy, and useless.

### Pace yourself:
- **Rest when she rests.** She'll have quiet windows between contractions, especially early. Sit. Close your eyes. You don't need to hover.
- **Take bathroom breaks during her calm periods**, not during a contraction run.
- **Trade off if you have a second support person** — you're the primary, but even 15 minutes in the hallway resets you.
- **Know your own tell.** If you feel lightheaded or panicky, step back, breathe, eat something, and return. Better a 2-minute break than a collapse at a critical moment.

### The 3 a.m. moment:
At some point you'll be exhausted, hungry, and standing in a fluorescent hallway, and it will feel endless. That's normal. The fix: one tiny task. Water. A walk to the window. Ten slow breaths. Then back in — because the version of you who returns is the one she needs.

## ONE LAST THING

You are about to witness something extraordinary, and you get to be the person she holds onto while it happens. You don't need to be brave or perfect. You just need to show up, stay calm, and keep saying: **"I'm right here."** That's the whole job — and you're ready for it.
