## 2. What Saturday's Visit Means for the Playbook

Three facts from the 36w 6d checkup, translated into this week's actions:

**GBS swab done, result pending.** When it lands (usually 2–4 days): *positive* → write "GBS positive — IV antibiotics in labour, aim to head in in good time" on the birth plan (see Section 4 — GBS-positive is one of the two reasons to leave earlier rather than later). *Negative* → write "GBS negative" and forget about it. Either way, the birth plan stays one page and the plan stays the same: vaginal, unmedicated, same people in the room.

**Head down, face to her right.** The doctor described the position plainly, so let's take it plainly: the head is down (the only orientation that matters for a vaginal birth), and the face is turned toward her right side rather than tucked toward her spine. At 37 weeks this is an ordinary finding — babies in transverse head positions commonly rotate to face-down (occiput anterior) during labour itself, and the rotation is often what the early hours of labour are *doing*. There is no corrective exercise to add. What supports it is what you're already doing: the asymmetric pelvis work (curb walking) gives the baby room to turn, hands-and-knees rocking keeps the pelvis unloaded, and the deep squat keeps the outlet open. **Do not bolt on a "turning routine"** — aggressive inversion schedules and internet spinning protocols at 37 weeks trade anxiety for effort. Mention it at the 20 Oct visit if you want the doctor's read, but the plan doesn't change.

**Admission = the usual card.** The last paperwork mystery is solved: no separate admission letter from Dr Paul exists or is needed. She brings her usual antenatal card to TMC admissions. The hospital-bag checklist loses its last open item — and he should confirm the card is *in the bag*, not somewhere it has to be found at 3 a.m.

## 3. The Call — Who Calls, When, and What to Say

In every scenario below, **he** is the one who calls. She is busy. His job from the first uncertain contraction to the car ride is to be the calm interface between her body and the outside world.

### The 5-1-1 rule (the standard head-in threshold)

**Contractions 5 minutes apart, each lasting 1 minute, sustained for 1 hour → call, and go in.** This is the textbook version. Two refinements from the program:

- **Confirm *your* provider's version** — at the 20 Oct visit (~38w 2d), ask Dr Paul's team directly: "What contraction pattern do you want us to head in on?" Some providers prefer 4-1-1 or 3-1-1 for first labours given the drive. Write the answer in the antenatal booklet. Until then, 5-1-1 stands.
- **The rule describes active labour's *arrival*, not its approach.** You'll spend hours in early labour at home *before* 5-1-1. That's the design (Section 4), not a delay.

### Call now, whatever the clock says — the four exceptions

| Situation | What it is | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Water breaks, no contractions** | A gush or steady trickle of fluid. Note the **time** and the **colour**: clear/pale = normal; **green/brown (meconium)** or foul-smelling = call promptly | His first-5-actions protocol is [`LABOUR-DAY-GUIDE.md`](../../program/LABOUR-DAY-GUIDE.md) §2.3 — time + colour first, then call. Most providers want you in sooner after ROM even without contractions; ask at the visit |
| **Bright red bleeding** | Period-like flow, not pink-tinged mucus (that's bloody show — an announcement, not an alarm) | Call immediately |
| **Reduced or changed movement** | Fewer kicks than her normal pattern, or a pattern that changes suddenly | Call immediately. Movement changes are always a call — no waiting, no "let's see" |
| **Severe headache, visual changes, upper-abdominal pain, sudden face/hand swelling, fever** | Possible pre-eclampsia or infection signs | Call immediately |

**What to say on the call (his script):** name, EDD, "we're X weeks", what's happening, for how long, the pattern if timed, water status, GBS status once known, "what do you want us to do?" Then he relays to her in one sentence. No decisions made on the phone beyond *go now / stay and call back at X*.

## 4. Early Labour at Home — The Longest Phase, the Most Mismanaged

This is the whole of the discipline, and it's worth saying straight: **the couples who struggle most are usually not the ones with hard labours — they're the ones who went to the hospital too early.** Arriving at 2 cm in a first labour buys hours under fluorescent lights, on a monitor, being offered interventions for a labour that was progressing exactly as designed. Early labour at home isn't toughing it out — it's *protecting* the labour from the environment that slows it down.

### The hour-by-hour shape of it

Early (latent) labour in a first birth commonly lasts **6–12 hours, sometimes longer**. It does not look like the movies. It looks like this:

- **Hours 0–3 — "Is this it?"** Contractions are irregular, mild, period-like, felt mostly in front. She can talk through them, maybe laugh between them. **What to do:** nothing special. Note the start time. Eat a real meal (she'll need the fuel — the uterus is about to run a marathon). Drink water. If it's night, *try to sleep* — doze between contractions. The anxiety of "is this really it?" is the signature feeling of this phase; the answer is always "maybe, and it doesn't matter yet."
- **Hours 3–6 — "Okay, this is a pattern."** Contractions get more regular, a little stronger, don't fully ease with rest or a position change. **What to do:** he starts timing quietly (an app, not a stopwatch held over her). She walks, showers, leans on the birth ball. Light snacks continue — honey, dates, fruit, toast. The rule: **if she can sleep, sleep; if she can't, move gently.**
- **Hours 6+ — "This is real and it's getting serious."** Contractions need her attention, she's quieter, breathing changes, she may want the shower or the ball continuously. **What to do:** keep the 5-1-1 (or your provider's pattern) in view. When the pattern holds for the full hour — call, then go. Pack the car *before* this point (the bag has been by the door since week 36 — this is what that was for).

### The early-labour rules

1. **Eat and drink.** Labour is an athletic event and she hasn't eaten since it started. Small, frequent, carbohydrate-rich: honey, dates, coconut water, fruit, toast. Dehydration mimics stalled labour.
2. **Rest is productive.** Every doze between contractions in early labour is banked energy for transition. Lying down is not "giving up" — it's strategy.
3. **Water helps more than almost anything.** A warm shower or bath in early labour measurably eases pain for most women. (Once waters have broken, showers yes, baths only per your provider's guidance.)
4. **He times, she doesn't.** She should never be watching the clock. He tracks the pattern and announces only what matters: "they're getting closer" or "let's keep going."
5. **Don't perform labour for an audience.** This is the week to set the boundary (Session 15 covers the "any news???" texts): nobody needs live updates. Every update costs her focus.

### The two reasons to leave earlier than 5-1-1

- **GBS-positive** (if Saturday's swab says so): antibiotics work best started well before delivery, so the plan is to head in in good time rather than labouring at home to the last comfortable minute.
- **Water broke first with no contractions**: most providers want you in sooner; confirm the exact instruction at the 20 Oct visit.

## 5. Arriving at TMC — Triage, the Exam, and "You're Only 3 cm"

Walking in prepared removes the fear of the unknown, which is most of what triage anxiety is.

**What happens, in order:** Admissions (the card — Section 2), then triage: a midwife takes her history, checks vitals, listens to the baby, and does a **vaginal examination** to assess dilation, effacement, and station — the Bishop components from the week-36 guide. There may be a period of **monitoring** (CTG) to check the baby's heart rate against contractions. Then a decision: admitted to the labour ward, or (if it's very early) sent home to continue labouring.

**"You're only 3 cm."** If you hear this, translate it before reacting: 3 cm with a soft, effacing cervix and a baby descending *is* labour, and it is going well. The number is a snapshot, not a verdict. The calm response — his line, rehearsed in week 35 — is *"Can we have ten minutes to talk it through?"* and then the two of you decide: walk the corridors for an hour and be rechecked, or go home if they advise it. Going home at 3 cm is not failure; it's the system working. What you don't do is hear "only" as a judgment and let it set the emotional tone of the next ten hours.

**Setting up the room (his job, first 10 minutes):** dim the lights, music on low if she wants it, minimal people, the affirmation cards where she can see them, water and honey sticks within his reach (not hers to manage). The room should feel like a place to labour, not a place to be processed. Every intervention offered from here gets the same three questions (week 35's advocacy lines): *"Let me check with her." "We need ten minutes." "Is this an emergency, or can we talk it through?"*

## 6. Active Labour — Positions, Breathing, and His Full-Time Job

Active labour is the phase the whole program has been rehearsing since week 33. The physiology: contractions every 3–5 minutes, lasting ~60 seconds, dilation from ~4–6 cm toward complete. It is hard, and it is *designed* — each surge is the uterus doing exactly what the last 37 weeks prepared it for.

### Position changes every ~30 minutes

Staying in one position for hours is the most common avoidable mistake in hospital labour. Rotation keeps the pelvis opening in different planes and keeps the baby descending:

- **Upright and moving:** walking the corridor, slow dancing with him, standing lean on the bed
- **Hands and knees:** on the bed or a mat — takes pressure off the back, gives the baby room (and room to rotate — Section 2's note on position)
- **Birth ball:** sitting, leaning over it, hip circles between surges
- **Side-lying:** with a pillow between the knees — the rest position; use it when she's tired, it still counts as labouring
- **Deep squat / supported squat:** the position her body has rehearsed since week 25 — opens the outlet; use it in bursts, not marathons

He is the one who suggests the change — *"want to try the ball for a bit?"* — because she won't think of it mid-surge.

### Breathing and sound

The program's breathing since week 27 has had one rule: **exhale on effort, never hold the breath.** In active labour that becomes: slow breath in through the nose, long audible breath out through the mouth — the "oooooh" and "aaaaah" she's been vocalizing in the squat for weeks. Low sounds keep the jaw loose; **tight jaw = tight pelvic floor** (week 36's cheapest high-value drill, now paying out). If her sounds go high and tight, his three-word correction: *"Drop your jaw."*

### His full-time job, restated

He does not watch the monitor. He does not time anything anymore. He stays in her eyeline, breathes with her, keeps the water coming, suggests positions, guards the room (dim, quiet, few people), and runs interference on every decision with the advocacy lines. The week-35 coaching manual is the reference; the week-36 final rehearsal was the last drill. He knows this.

## 7. Transition — The 20–60 Minutes of "I Can't Do This"

Transition (roughly 7–10 cm) is the shortest phase and the one every birth story is really about. Contractions come every 2–3 minutes, last 60–90 seconds, with barely a break between. This is where she may say — verbatim, and it means nothing is wrong — *"I can't do this," "I want to go home," "give me the epidural,"* or go quiet in a way that scares him.

**What's happening:** the cervix is finishing the last, fastest dilation; the baby is descending; her body is flooding with adrenaline and endorphins at once. The feelings are *chemical*, not informational. "I can't do this" at transition is so standard that experienced midwives hear it as a progress report: it usually means the pushing stage is close.

**What he does:** this is the phase the program has rehearsed since week 33, and the rules are the simplest of the whole birth:

1. **Don't fix it, don't leave.** Hand on her back, breathing with her, saying almost nothing.
2. **Three-word sentences only:** *"You're doing it." "One at a time." "I'm right here."*
3. **Don't offer the epidural she asked not to want** — unless she asks three times, clearly, between surges, and means it. One mention during a surge is the drugs talking, not her. (This was the birth plan's line; honour it the way she asked you to.)
4. **Tell her what's happening:** *"This is transition. This is the shortest part. You're nearly there."* Information is calming when the body feels out of control.

Then it's over — often suddenly — and the urge to push arrives like a gear change.

## 8. Pushing — The Urge, the Positions, the Ring of Fire

**Follow the urge, don't force it.** The program's pushing line since week 25: wait for the overwhelming, involuntary bearing-down urge (it feels like the strongest need to have a bowel movement of her life), then push *with* it. **Open-glottis pushing** — breathe through it, exhale or vocalize while bearing down, don't hold the breath and strain. Directed, breath-holding, "push to ten" coaching is the older style; the evidence and the program both favour spontaneous, urge-driven pushing.

**Positions:** upright is the theme — squat (the rehearsed position), hands-and-knees, side-lying. She'll find the one; his job is to support it physically (hold her, brace her) and not suggest she lie flat unless she wants to.

**Crowning and the ring of fire:** as the head crowns, the perineum stretches to its maximum — a burning, stinging sensation that has a dramatic name and a reassuring meaning: *the head is nearly out, and the burning means the tissue is stretching, not tearing.* Slow, panting breaths here (not pushing hard) let the head emerge gradually — this is where the perineal massage and the release-breathing practice pay out. Then the head is out, the body follows usually within a contraction or two, and it's over.

**How many pushes?** Anywhere from a handful to a couple of hours in a first birth — both are normal. **If she needs to stop pushing briefly** (to pant through crowning, to rest, because the provider asks): stopping is always allowed. Nobody pushes continuously; the urge comes in waves and the rests between are part of it.

## 9. The Moment — Baby Out, On Her Chest

Then: the baby is out, and straight onto her chest — **skin-to-skin, immediately, uninterrupted**. This is the golden hour from the week-32 guide: the baby's first cry, the first look, her hands on wet skin, his hand on both of them. The cord keeps pulsing — **delayed cord clamping** (the birth plan's line; a minute or more while it pulses) lets the baby receive its full blood volume. The first latch often happens in this hour, unhurried, with the week-32 feeding station waiting at home for everything after.

**The placenta** follows — usually within 5–30 minutes, often with one or two more mild contractions and a feeling of pressure. It's the third stage, it's managed by the provider, and then the birth is, medically, complete. She did it.

**His job in the golden hour:** phone away, hands on them, witness it. Take the photos *after* the first hour if photos matter — the first hour belongs to the three of them, not the camera roll.

## 10. If Things Don't Go to Plan — The Two Detours

An unmedicated plan is not a fragile plan. Knowing the detours in advance is what keeps them from feeling like failures.

### Induction (the week-36 conversation, continued)

If the 41-week policy ever activates — or if there's a medical reason earlier — here's the honest difference: **induced labour often starts faster and feels more intense from the beginning**, because prostaglandin/oxytocin induction doesn't give you the long, gentle early phase that spontaneous labour does. Contractions can come on strong with less buildup, and continuous monitoring is more likely, which constrains movement.

How the playbook adapts: everything in Sections 4–9 still applies — positions (as movement allows), breathing, vocalization, his advocacy, the room setup. The one addition: **ask for the Bishop score** (week 36, §6) before induction starts. A favourable cervix (≥8) means induction usually behaves much like spontaneous labour; an unfavourable one (≤6) means ripening first, a longer process, and a higher chance of the cascade — which is worth knowing *before* you consent, not during. And the unmedicated goal doesn't evaporate with induction: many induced labours are still unmedicated. It just takes more of everything the program built.

### Emergency C-section

If at any point the provider says the baby needs to come out now and vaginal birth isn't safe: **this is the plan working, not the plan failing.** What happens: consent, quick prep, spinal anaesthesia (she's awake and numb from the chest down), a drape, pressure and tugging sensations but no pain, the baby out within minutes of the incision — and then, in most cases, **the baby goes straight to her chest** while they finish. Ask for it explicitly if it's not offered: *"Can we do skin-to-skin in theatre?"* Delayed cord clamping is often still possible — ask.

His job doesn't change: stay at her head, hold her hand, narrate what's happening, be the first to say *"he's here, he's beautiful."* The golden hour can still happen — skin-to-skin in recovery, the first latch within the hour. A surgical birth is still *her* birth, and the first-week guides (course chapters 8–10) were written for all of it.

## 11. The 5 Things NOT to Do in Labour

Session 14's follow-up prompt, answered straight:

1. **Don't go to the hospital in early labour.** The single most consequential decision of the birth. Home until the pattern holds (Section 4) — the hospital is for active labour.
2. **Don't lie flat on your back for hours.** It's the worst position for pelvic opening and the one hospital beds default to. Upright, side-lying, hands-and-knees — rotate.
3. **Don't hold your breath to push.** Breath-holding spikes pressure without helping the baby descend and exhausts her faster. Exhale, vocalize, ride the urge.
4. **Don't make decisions between surges.** Any offer — breaking waters, augmentation, pain relief — gets *"we need ten minutes"* and a discussion in a calm minute, never mid-contraction. (True emergencies excepted, and you'll know one if you see one.)
5. **Don't narrate the clock.** "It's been six hours" helps no one. Time is his quiet background task, never her foreground. The only numbers that matter are the ones the provider measures.

## ⚠️ When to Call, Not Read

**Call TMC Maternity / your OB on-call immediately — day or night — for:** regular contractions before 37 weeks (no longer applicable from Sunday — you're early term) · water breaking (time + colour — green/brown or foul-smelling needs a prompt call) · bright red bleeding · reduced or changed fetal movement · severe headache with visual changes, upper-abdominal pain, sudden face/hand swelling, or fever · a gut feeling that something is wrong. **A false alarm is the correct outcome of that call, every time.**
