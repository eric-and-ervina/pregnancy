## 1. Why Position Matters: The Pelvis Is Not a Fixed Ring

### The bones move

The pelvis is not one solid bone — it is four bones (two ilia, sacrum, coccyx) joined by flexible ligaments, loosened further by relaxin. When you change position, the **diameters** of the pelvic inlet and outlet literally change:

- **Squatting / kneeling forward** swings the ilia outward and flexes the sacrum back, **widening the outlet**. Classic radiographic work (Russell, 1969, updated 1982) measured the squatting position increasing the outlet by ~1 cm in the transverse diameter and ~2 cm in the antero-posterior diameter — roughly a **28% larger outlet area** than supine. The exact number matters less than the mechanism: **your position can make the exit bigger.**
- **Lying flat on your back** presses the sacral base into the bed and tilts the inlet so baby has to climb "uphill" against gravity while navigating the *narrowest* configuration of the ring.
- **Lithotomy (back, legs in stirrups)** is the worst of it: the sacrum is pinned against the mattress (no posterior swing), gravity is subtracted entirely, the outlet is at its smallest, and the birth canal is forced into an upward curve. It survives in hospitals for provider convenience during assisted delivery — not because it helps you.

### What the evidence actually says

The Cochrane review of maternal positions during second stage ([Gupta et al., 2017](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD002006.pub4/full)) found that upright positions (sitting, squatting, kneeling) versus lying down were associated with:

- a **slightly shorter second stage** (~6 minutes on average — a small, low-certainty effect that disappears when higher-bias trials are excluded),
- **fewer instrumental (vacuum/forceps) deliveries** (~25% relative reduction, moderate-certainty),
- **fewer episiotomies**,
- but more **blood loss >500 mL** and a possible increase in **second-degree tears** (both findings of low-to-moderate certainty, and upright does not raise the risk of third/fourth-degree tears).

Read that honestly: upright is not magic and it is not free. It trades a bit of bleeding/tearing risk for less intervention — and for a natural-birth plan, keeping the vacuum and forceps off the table is usually the trade you want. (Perineal massage from Week 34, in [`PROGRAM.md`](../../program/PROGRAM.md), is the planned answer to the tearing side of the ledger.)

### Position is also pain management

Every position change does two things beyond mechanics: it **redistributes pressure** (no single spot on your body absorbs every surge) and it gives your brain a **job** (the Week 30 lesson — a busy mind is not a frightened mind). Staying mobile in early labor is one of the few interventions that reliably *shortens* the boring part of labor and delays the moment you'd otherwise be asking for an epidural just to escape the bed.

## 2. The Cardinal Movements: What Baby Is Doing Down There

Your positions are half the story. Baby's rotations are the other half. Labor is a two-body negotiation with the pelvis, and each of baby's seven "cardinal movements" is helped by specific things you do:

| # | Movement | What it means | What helps |
|---|----------|---------------|------------|
| 1 | **Engagement** | Head settles into the inlet | Upright posture, walking, good posture all 3rd trimester |
| 2 | **Descent** | Head moves down | Gravity: upright, kneeling, squatting |
| 3 | **Flexion** | Chin tucks to chest (smallest diameter presents) | Forward lean; open hip positions |
| 4 | **Internal rotation** | Head turns ~90° to fit the outlet | Lunges, hip circles, walking the halls — asymmetric movement gives the head room to twist |
| 5 | **Extension** | Head pivots out under the pubic bone | Controlled, unhurried pushing; upright or hands-and-knees (no forced pushing) |
| 6 | **Restitution / external rotation** | Head realigns with shoulders | Hands off — baby does this alone |
| 7 | **Expulsion** | Shoulders and body follow | Position changes; typically the easy part |

The practical takeaway: **asymmetric, rotating movement (lunges, circles, dancing) is what powers internal rotation** — the step where back-labor babies most often get stuck. If you remember one line from this table, remember that.

## 3. Positions by Labor Stage

> Dilation numbers are a guide, not a scoreboard. Cervixes do not read textbooks — go by the *character* of the surges and your instinct, then confirm with the midwife.

### Early labor (0–6 cm) — the mobility phase

**Goal: stay upright, stay home, stay distracted.** This stage can last many hours (especially a first baby) and ends faster when you treat it like a normal day with a soundtrack of surges.

- **Walking** — the default. Gravity + hip sway = engagement and rotation.
- **Slow dancing with your partner** — swaying, his hands on your hips. Pain relief and progress in one.
- **Shower** — warm water on your lower back or belly; labor's cheapest analgesic.
- **Birth ball** (if available) — sitting, gentle bouncing, hip circles.
- **Rest positions** — side-lying with a pillow between the knees when fatigue demands it. Sleep if you can sleep; early labor often offers windows of dozing. Do not "push through" exhaustion at 2 cm — you will want that reserve later.

**Do not** lie flat on your back, and do not begin "laboring" in the bed out of politeness. Beds slow early labor.

### Active labor (6–8 cm) — the working phase

Surges are now strong, 3–5 minutes apart, lasting ~60 seconds. Conversation is over. **Goal: keep baby rotating and descending; use asymmetric positions.**

- **Hands-and-knees** — the workhorse. Takes pressure off the back, lets the belly hang (baby gravitates toward your spine — great if she is facing the wrong way), and is the single best position for back labor.
- **Lunges** — side lunge with one foot on a chair; opens one side of the pelvis at a time so the head can rotate. Switch sides every few surges.
- **Supported squat** — partner or squat bar support; the widest-outlet position (Section 1). Hold for a surge at a time; it is intense.
- **Birth ball** — circles and figure-8s, leaning forward over it if you want the hands-and-knees benefit with less wrist strain.
- **Leaning forward standing** — against the bed, a wall, or your partner's shoulders.

### Transition (8–10 cm) — the storm

Surges are nearly continuous and the "I can't do this" feeling arrives — which, usefully, is often the signal that you are almost done. **Goal: support, not effort.** Nobody expects heroics here.

- **Side-lying** — pillows between knees and under the top arm; the rest position that still leaves the pelvis free. The default when you are spent.
- **Hands-and-knees, chest on a pile of pillows** — knees low, chest low, hips high. Deeply restful version of the workhorse.
- **Supported squat** — if you feel an urge to bear down *before* full dilation, a supported squat or side-lying position (rather than holding your breath) lets you ride the urge without pushing against an unready cervix. Tell the midwife — she will check.
- **Peanut ball** (if the hospital has one) — between the knees in side-lying; keeps the pelvis open while you genuinely rest.

### Pushing — follow the urge, follow the baby

The old image — flat on back, legs in stirrups, counting to ten, purple-faced pushing — is the lithotomy problem again. What works better:

| Position | Pros | Cons / notes |
|----------|------|--------------|
| **Upright squat** (supported, bar or partner) | Widest outlet + gravity + shortest pushes | Legs tire fast; use in bursts |
| **Hands-and-knees** | Great for big babies / back labor; perineum relaxes; tears may be fewer | Midwife access for exams awkward (she will manage) |
| **Side-lying** | Restful; slow controlled delivery of the head; gentle on the perineum | No gravity assist — fine, pushing is mostly uterus now |
| **Semi-reclined** (head of bed up ~45°, "supported throne") | Familiar, good for slow landings with the partner behind | Keep it *semi* — not flat |

Two rules beat any position choice: **push with the urge, not the count** (spontaneous pushing is gentler on the perineum and just as effective), and **change position if you stall** — a stall in pushing is often solved by a move, not by more force.

## 4. The Labor Dance: Movement as a Rotational Tool

This is the Week 31 signature skill, and it is exactly what it sounds like — slow dancing, but the dance floor is your pelvis:

- **Slow sway** — standing, partner's hands on your hips or your arms around his neck; sway side to side through a surge. The sway transfers your weight hip to hip, nudging baby through each rotation.
- **Hip circles** — big slow circles, like stirring a pot with your hips, one direction for a few minutes, then the other.
- **Figure-8s** — the same, but drawing a lazy "8" on the floor. Figure-8s combine the two directions of pelvic tilt at once and are the single best movement for encouraging internal rotation.

All three are already in your daily routine (birth-ball substitutes and deep-squat work in [`physical.md`](physical.md) train the same joints). The birth-day version just adds a partner and a playlist. One practical note: **low, deep vocalization while you move** — the "oooooh"/"aaaaah" you are practicing this week — keeps your pelvic floor from clenching. A dropped jaw is a dropped pelvic floor.

## 5. Equipment: What Exists, What It Does

Confirm with the hospital what is actually available (put it on the birth-preferences list); do not count on any of it being in the room by default.

| Tool | What it's for | How it's used |
|------|---------------|---------------|
| **Birth ball** | Sitting, circles, leaning | Inflate firm; feet wide; a birthing ball, not a gym ball. Circles during active labor; lean-over during back labor |
| **Peanut ball** | Resting with the pelvis open | Between knees/ankles in side-lying — the transition and epidural-companion MVP |
| **Squat bar** | Supported pushing | Mounts on the bed; you pull into the squat, arms take the leg effort out |
| **Rebozo** (long woven cloth) | Hip support & gentle shaking | Partner uses it to gently sift/shake the hips (releases tension), or to support the belly in hands-and-knees. Ask a doula to demo — 3 techniques in 10 minutes |
| **Shower** | Warm water analgesia | Direct the stream at the sacrum (back labor) or belly; stay for several surges, partner manages towels |
| **Tub** | Immersion comfort | Evidence suggests immersion in active labor reduces pain and epidural use. Get in when surges are well established (~5–6 cm is common guidance); get out if monitoring is needed, labor slows, or you feel faint |

**Water honesty check:** immersion reliably improves *comfort* and may modestly reduce epidural use; it does not make contractions weaker. That is still a win — comfort keeps adrenaline down, and Week 30 taught you what adrenaline does to labor.

## 6. If You're in the Hospital Bed Anyway

Sometimes monitoring, exhaustion, or simple logistics put you in the bed. The bed is not a defeat — it is a position to be *modified*:

- **Side-lying + peanut/pillows** — the default hospital-bed position. Knees bent, peanut (or 2–3 pillows) between them, top leg forward. Pelvis stays open; you can actually rest.
- **Hands-and-knees on the bed** — completely legitimate. Knees on the mattress, chest on stacked pillows, hips above knees. Ask for the bed's backrest down.
- **Kneeling, leaning on the raised backrest** — the bed becomes a leaning post; you are still upright.
- **Semi-reclined ≠ flat** — if you must be on your back (e.g., during monitoring), raise the head of the bed and drop a wedge/pillow under one hip to tip the sacrum off the mattress. A tilted back is not a pinned back.
- **The "throne"** — head of bed fully up, sitting on the edge of a chair-less bed with feet on a stool. Undignified, effective.

Every one of these beats flat lithotomy — and the midwives at a busy unit have seen all of them a thousand times. Asking is normal.

## 7. The Position Flowchart: "If I'm Feeling X, Try Y"

Print this. Put it in the hospital bag. Your partner's job on the day is to read it when you cannot:

| If you're feeling… | Try… | Because… |
|--------------------|------|----------|
| **"My back is killing me"** (back labor) | Hands-and-knees; hip circles; shower on the sacrum; partner's counter-pressure (Week 29 guide) | Baby is likely facing your spine (OP); these positions rotate her and open the sacrum |
| **"Labor is slowing down"** | Walk the halls; stair steps; lunge series; rest if it's the middle of the night and you're only ~2–3 cm | Movement re-engages baby; sometimes the stall is fatigue asking for a 20-minute nap |
| **"I can't get comfortable in any position"** | Change *something* every 2–3 surges: ball → knee → stand → side-lying | Relentless discomfort usually means one body part is taking all the pressure |
| **"The surges are unbearable"** (active labor) | Shower/tub; supported squat for one surge at a time; slow dance; low "aaaaah" vocalization | Warm water + vocalization + supported positions lower the adrenaline loop |
| **"I'm exhausted, I need to rest"** (transition) | Side-lying with peanut/pillows; hands-and-knees chest-on-pillows | Rest positions that keep the pelvis open; you are closer than you feel |
| **"I feel like I need to push" before full dilation** | Supported squat or side-lying; breathe *through* the urge; tell the midwife | Riding the urge gently protects an unready cervix |
| **"Pushing isn't moving the baby"** | Change position (side-lying ↔ squat ↔ hands-and-knees); rest between urges | A stall is usually a rotation problem, not a strength problem |
| **"I'm stuck in the bed for monitoring"** | Side-lying + peanut; tilted-back semi-reclined; kneeling on the bed | Bed modifications beat flat-on-back every time |

## 8. Cervical Dilation & the Latent Phase — When to Actually Go In

The education items in [`PROGRAM.md`](../../program/PROGRAM.md) this week: know what dilation means, and know when to leave home.

- **Latent phase (0–~4 cm):** contractions irregular, mild-to-moderate, often hours of "is this it?" — best managed at home: eat, rest, shower, walk. Going to the hospital at 2 cm mostly buys you a corridor and a possible send-home.
- **The 5-1-1 rule** (use it as a guide, not a law): contractions **5** minutes apart, each lasting about **1** minute, sustained for **1** hour — time to head in (call first, per your provider's instructions).
- **Go sooner, regardless of the rule, if:** waters break (note the time and color — clear is fine; green/brown means meconium and means now), bleeding beyond spotting, decreased fetal movement, or instinct says something is wrong.
- **Active labor (~6 cm onward)** is when positions from Section 3's "active" list earn their keep — that is the phase you are training for.

A full minute-by-minute timeline of the whole labor is Session 10 (Week 33) — this week you only need the front door.

## 9. Rehearsal Plan: How to Actually Use This Week

Reading is not rehearsal. This week, fold positions into what you are already doing ([`physical.md`](physical.md)):

1. **During deep-squat holds** — practice one surge's worth of slow breathing (Week 30 pattern C) *in* the squat. Squat + surge breath is your pushing rehearsal.
2. **During hip circles / curb walking** — add the low "oooooh"/"aaaaah" vocalization. Jaw relaxed, sound low and open.
3. **Hands-and-knees for 2 minutes daily** — during the cat-cow block, stay up and breathe through two "surge-length" minutes (60-second waves).
4. **Side-lying with pillows between the knees** — once this week, lie there for 10 minutes doing Sleep Breathing. This is your transition position; make it familiar now.
5. **The labor dance with your partner** — put on one slow song, sway, hip circles, figure-8s. Three minutes. It will feel silly. Do it anyway; the silliness is why you'll remember it.
6. **Draft the flowchart card** (Section 7) — print it or copy it onto one page and put it in the hospital bag. Have your partner read it once aloud; that single read-through is most of his prep for positions.
