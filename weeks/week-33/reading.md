> **👶 Baby this week (per [`THIRD-TRIMESTER-GUIDE.md`](../../program/THIRD-TRIMESTER-GUIDE.md)):** ~4.2 lb / 1.9 kg (pineapple) · immune system developing — your antibodies are crossing the placenta now · bones hardening, but the skull stays soft and overlapping for birth · sleeping in REM cycles.

Here is the thing about labor: everyone prepares for it in pieces. A breathing pattern here, a position there, a class on Tuesdays. And then the day arrives and the pieces have to run *simultaneously, in sequence, for many hours*, with a woman who has been awake since 3 a.m. Week 33's job is to put the pieces in order. Not to make labor predictable — it isn't — but to make it **legible**. When you know that hours five through nine are supposed to feel exactly like this, and that the scary part is short and means you're nearly done, fear has much less to work with.

Read this once for the story, then keep Section 10 (the printable timeline) somewhere you'll actually find it.

## 2. The Shape of Labor: Five Phases at a Glance

This is the map. Every section after it is a zoom-in.

| Phase | Cervix | Typical length (first baby) | Contractions | What she feels | Where she should be |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Pre-labor** | 0 cm → effacing, softening | Days to weeks | Irregular; don't get closer or stronger; fade with rest | Period-like ache, low back heaviness, tightening that stops when distracted | Home, normal life |
| **Early / Latent** | 0 → 6 cm | **6–12 h, often longer** | 5–20 min apart → 5 min; 30–60 s; mild → moderate | Cramping wave, backache, "I can still talk through it" | **Home** — rest, eat, walk, shower |
| **Active** | 6 → 8 cm | ~2–5 h | 3–5 min apart; 45–60 s; strong | Must stop and breathe; can't talk through it; needs support | Hospital (5-1-1 → go) |
| **Transition** | 8 → 10 cm | **15 min – 1.5 h** | 1.5–3 min apart; 60–90 s; back-to-back | Overwhelming pressure, nausea, shaking, "I can't do this" | Hospital, one room, dim lights |
| **Pushing** | 10 cm → birth | 30 min – 3 h (~1 h average) | Unmistakable, involuntary urge to push | Enormous pressure, then the "ring of fire" at crowning | Hospital, upright if possible |
| **Placenta** | — | 5–30 min | A few mild contractions | Relief. Cramping, a push, done | Same room — on your chest with baby |

Two things to notice about that table. First, **the longest phase is the one you spend at home** — by design. Second, **the hardest phase is the shortest.** Transition gets all the drama of a season finale and lasts about as long as an episode.

## 3. Pre-Labor: The Signs That Come Days (or Hours) Before

The body runs a quiet countdown before labor proper. None of these signs is a scheduling announcement; several can appear a fortnight early and mean nothing about *when*.

| Sign | What it actually is | What to do | When to call |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lightening / "the baby dropped"** | Baby's head settles into the pelvis. Breathing gets easier, pelvic pressure and bathroom trips get worse | Enjoy the breathing. Expect more waddling, more pelvic pressure | Not a call on its own |
| **Nesting urge** | A sudden need to reorganize cupboards at 11 p.m. Hormonal and real | Do a *little*. Do not move furniture. This is a classic trap for overexertion | Not a call |
| **Mucus plug** | A jelly-like blob (clear, pinkish, or streaked) passes as the cervix softens | Note it, wear a liner, carry on | Only if it's bright red bleeding |
| **Bloody show** | Pink/brown-tinged mucus — capillaries breaking as the cervix opens | Normal. Usually hours to days before labor | If bleeding is heavy/bright red → **go in** |
| **Braxton Hicks intensify** | Practice contractions get more frequent and noticeable | Hydrate, change position, rest. They should fade | If they become regular, timeable, and close together |
| **Water breaking** | Membrane rupture — a gush *or* a continuous trickle you can't stop | Note **time** and **colour**. Pad up. Nothing in the vagina | **Call TMC Maternity immediately — go in even without contractions** |
| **Loose stools / "clearing out"** | Prostaglandins loosen the gut in the day or two before labor | Normal, expected | Not a call |
| **Reduced fetal movement** | *Not* a pre-labor sign — a red flag | — | **Call immediately. Never wait for the next appointment.** |

**The colour rule for water breaking**, because it's the one detail families forget under stress: **clear or slightly pink is normal**; **green/brown means meconium** (baby has passed stool) — tell the hospital and follow their instruction to come in; **bright red bleeding is an emergency.** Note the time either way; the infection-clock conversation starts from that timestamp.

> ⚠️ **The Week 33 caveat, and it matters this week specifically:** you are 33 weeks. **Any** regular, timeable contractions now — along with any bleeding, any fluid leak, any change in baby's movement pattern — is a **call-now**, not a note-for-later. Preterm labor signs before 37 weeks are [CASE ③ in `LABOUR-DAY-GUIDE.md`](../../program/LABOUR-DAY-GUIDE.md) and the threshold there is deliberately low. The timeline below describes a term labor. At 33 weeks, you call early and you call often.

## 4. Early / Latent Labor (0–6 cm): The Longest Phase, Mostly at Home

This is where most of your preparation gets spent, and where most of the avoidable interventions are won or lost. Latent labor is **slow by nature**: the cervix isn't just opening, it's *thinning and softening* — from a firm inch to a stretchy, paper-thin rim. The old "1 cm per hour" rule was never a description of normal latent labor, and labor that *follows* it is the exception.

**What it feels like:** a low, period-like cramping wave that builds and releases, often with lower-back ache. There's still a gap between waves long enough to talk, joke, doze. Contractions in early labor are frequently 5–20 minutes apart and irregular — they can be 8 minutes, then 4, then 11.

**What you actually do:**

- **Stay home.** This is the single highest-leverage decision of the whole day. Arriving at hospital at 3 cm invites a timeline, a monitor, a "would you like something to move this along," and the intervention cascade from Week 28. Arriving at 6 cm invites a room and a midwife and no suggestions at all.
- **Rest, and eat.** Labor is an endurance event with a long opening leg. Sleep if you can — it is *not* a waste of progress; it's fuel for the last six hours. Eat real food: rice, eggs, toast, noodles, fruit. Drink to thirst (and keep drinking in later phases).
- **Move gently.** Walking, swaying, the birth ball, a warm shower, slow dancing with your partner, curb-walking. Upright and forward-leaning positions encourage baby to descend and rotate.
- **Note the pattern, don't interrogate it.** Time contractions in a notebook every so often — not every single one, and not on a stopwatch from 8 p.m. Under-support and over-timing are twin mistakes.
- **Protect the mood.** Dim lights, one room, no audience. Adrenaline from a crowded, excited house is a labor-stalling drug.

**When to go in — the 5-1-1 rule:** contractions **5** minutes apart, each lasting **1** minute, sustained for **1** hour. Plus the overrides:

| Go in immediately, regardless of 5-1-1 |
|---|
| Water has broken (note the time and colour) |
| Bright red bleeding |
| Reduced fetal movement |
| You can't reach your provider and something feels wrong |
| Any of the above at **under 37 weeks** — you are in that window now |

**If you arrive and you're "only 3 cm":** this is normal, not failure, and it is not a verdict. Ask for the option to walk the corridors, use the shower, or go home and return later — and if that isn't possible, this is exactly the moment the birth-preferences document exists for. The husband's script for this conversation is in [`LABOUR-DAY-GUIDE.md`](../../program/LABOUR-DAY-GUIDE.md) Phase 1 and Session 12's role-play in [`STUDY.md`](../../program/STUDY.md).

**Partner's job in early labor:** water, food, quiet company, one hand on her back, and *not* narrating. Time a few contractions. Tell her when the pattern crosses 5-1-1. Keep the bag by the door. Sleep in shifts if it's the middle of the night — there is a long road ahead and his alertness is a finite resource.

## 5. Active Labor (6–8 cm)

The gate from 6 cm onward is where modern obstetrics draws the line between "latent, however long it takes" and "active." ACOG's 2014 obstetric-care consensus did this deliberately: because the old threshold of 4 cm led to too many labors being labelled "arrested" during what is really still the slow phase, and too many first cesareans as a result.

**What changes:**

- Contractions get **longer, stronger, and closer** — 3–5 minutes apart, 45–60 seconds or more. There is no talking through them; conversation resumes in the gap and stops the moment the next wave begins.
- She's working now. This is the phase where every tool from Week 27's pain-management session gets used *in rotation*, not all at once.
- Progress also speeds up: after 6 cm, dilation typically accelerates, and the cervical change from here is often faster than the previous several hours combined.

**The toolkit, in rough order of what to try:**

| Tool | When it fits |
|---|---|
| Position changes every ~30 min | Always. Upright, forward-leaning, hands-and-knees, lunging, side-lying |
| Slow breathing, long exhale, low vocalisation | Every contraction, from the first one |
| Counter-pressure / double hip squeeze | From here on — this is where Week 29–31 practice pays |
| Shower or bath, if available | Best pain-relief-to-effort ratio in the room |
| Birth ball, peanut ball | Sitting, circling, leaning over; peanut ball in side-lying for rotation |
| Heat pack on the sacrum | Back-labour and lower-back ache |
| Cold cloth, sip of water, honey date | Between waves — recovery matters as much as coping |
| The epidural conversation | Only if *she* raises it. Know the options in advance; a decision made in the middle of a wave is not a decision |

**What the midwife is doing:** listening to baby intermittently, checking progress only when it's useful, and watching for the things nobody can see from the outside — baby's position and descent, her blood pressure, her hydration and bladder. Emptying her bladder every hour or two is a genuinely overlooked part of "progress."

## 6. Transition (8–10 cm): "I Can't Do This"

The shortest phase and the one everyone remembers. Transition is where the last two centimetres happen, and it arrives with a signature set of signs:

- Contractions **1.5–3 minutes apart**, lasting **60–90 seconds** — barely any recovery gap
- Pressure that feels like the whole pelvis is being reorganised
- **Nausea and vomiting**, hot-and-cold flushes, and shaking that she can't control
- Sudden despair, irritability, "make it stop," "I want the epidural," "I can't do this"

**"I can't do this" is not a crisis. It is a *sign*.** In practice, in most births, that sentence arrives in the final stretch — often within a handful of contractions of full dilation. It's the point where the tool of *explaining* is worthless and the only thing that works is presence.

**What actually helps in these 20–40 minutes:**

- **One voice, one face, close in.** Eye contact. Sentences of three words or fewer.
- **The honest reframe, stated calmly:** *"This is the hardest part and it is almost over. Your body is doing exactly what it should."*
- **Don't argue with the epidural request, and don't promise either.** Acknowledge it, ask the midwife what's happening, buy the decision ten minutes — in transition, ten minutes changes the whole picture.
- **Keep the room quiet.** Reduce talking to her; talk *to the nurse* on her behalf.
- **Jaw loose, throat open, sound low.** The low "oooooh" is doing mechanical work, not just emotional. A tight jaw is a tight pelvic floor.
- **Ride the wave imagery from Week 30** — this is the phase it was designed for.

If she's shaking and vomiting: that's the hormonal surge, not a bad reaction. Warm blanket, small sips, no commentary.

## 7. Pushing (10 cm → Birth)

Once she's fully dilated, the character of labor changes completely: from *enduring* to *doing*. Many women find pushing the most manageable phase of all, because there's finally something to push *against*.

**The urge:** a pressure so specific it's unmistakable — the sensation of needing to move the baby down and out, arriving with each contraction. Some women feel it immediately at 10 cm; others need a short rest first. Both are normal.

**How to push well:**

- **Wait for the urge, then follow it.** Spontaneous pushing — breathing down with the contraction rather than holding your breath against a coached count — is the approach in your birth-preferences document. Purple-faced breath-holding pushes are harder on both of you and don't shorten the phase.
- **Open glottis:** sound out, not locked in. Low "aaaaah" or a soft groan on the effort. Push *into* the exhale.
- **Positions:** upright squat or supported squat, hands-and-knees, side-lying, semi-reclined. Upright positions use gravity and widen the pelvic outlet; lying flat on your back in stirrups is the worst of the lot.
- **Rest completely between contractions.** Total flop: head down, jaw slack, eyes closed. Two minutes of real rest buys a much better next push.
- **Change positions every 20–30 minutes** if progress is slow — and consider a rest on hands-and-knees if she's exhausted.

**Crowning and the "ring of fire":** the moment the widest part of the baby's head stretches the perineum. It's intense and it's brief. The instinct is to clamp down; the instruction is the opposite — **stop pushing, breathe, let the tissue do what it's built to do.** Slow, controlled delivery of the head is the single most protective thing you can offer your perineum (perineal massage next week prepares the same tissue). Most providers will support the perineum with warm compresses and ask you to breathe the head out.

**How long:** ~1 hour on average for a first baby, with a wide normal range; the 95th percentile in a large modern dataset was about 2.8 hours without an epidural (3.6 with). Progress is counted in *descent and position*, not in pushes.

## 8. The Third Stage: Placenta, and the Hour You Already Planned

**The placenta** usually delivers within 5–30 minutes. You may feel a few mild contractions and a strange sense of something else to push. It's quick, and often barely noticed, because everyone in the room is looking at a baby.

**Then hour one begins — the golden hour you built in Week 32.** Skin-to-skin, undisturbed, delayed cord clamping if baby is vigorous, the nine stages of newborn behaviour, the first latch, and routine procedures deferred until after the first feed. If something needs to change for safety, the question stays the same: *"Is mom stable? Is baby stable?"* — then *"When can skin-to-skin start?"*

That whole hour is already written down in [`week-32/reading.md`](../week-32/reading.md) and in your birth-preferences document. This is why the timeline matters: it ends in a place you've already prepared.

## 9. How Long Is "Normal"? And What "Failure to Progress" Actually Means

**Total labor, first baby:** commonly quoted as 12–24 hours — but that number includes a latent phase that can stretch across a day or more. The largest modern study of spontaneous labor (Zhang et al., 2010, the Consortium on Safe Labor, ~62,000 births) reshaped the picture:

- **Before 6 cm, progress is slow and patient for everyone** — first-time and experienced mothers alike. The 95th percentile for dilating from 4 cm to 6 cm in first-time mothers was **nearly 10 hours**. That's a 19-in-20 event range, not a complication.
- **After 6 cm**, dilation typically accelerates — often the fastest part of the whole process.
- **Second stage 95th percentile:** ~2.8 hours without an epidural, ~3.6 hours with.

**"Failure to progress" / "arrest of labor"** is a real diagnosis, and it's also a phrase that has historically been applied too early and too readily — which is precisely why ACOG's 2014 guidance lowered the cesarean rate by redefining active labor as starting at 6 cm and requiring genuinely arrested progress (not merely slow progress) before calling it. Three questions worth knowing by heart, in plain language:

1. **"Is mom stable and is baby stable?"** If both are well, time is on your side.
2. **"Is this slow-but-progressing, or actually stopped?"** Slow is information. Stopped — no change over several hours with strong regular contractions — is a different conversation.
3. **"What's the benefit, and what's the alternative to waiting?"** Ask it about any proposed intervention, at any point. This is informed consent, and you are entitled to it every time.

**"Too long" becomes a medical concern when** baby isn't tolerating labor, mom is febrile or exhausted to the point of not coping, or there is genuinely no change in dilation or descent over a defined period despite adequate contractions. Those are real flags and they deserve real conversations — not panic, and not dismissal either. Session 10's follow-up prompts in [`STUDY.md`](../../program/STUDY.md) cover exactly this.

## ⚠️ When to Call, Not Read

**Call TMC Maternity / your OB on-call immediately — day or night — for:** any regular contractions before 37 weeks · water breaking · bright red bleeding · reduced or changed fetal movement · severe headache with visual changes or upper-abdominal pain · fever · a gut feeling that something is wrong. A false alarm is the correct outcome of that call, every time.
