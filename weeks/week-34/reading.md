## 2. The Anatomy, in Plain Language

**The perineum** is the region between the vaginal opening and the anus — a small diamond of skin, muscle and connective tissue that stretches to accommodate the baby's head in the last moments of birth. When the widest part of the head stretches it, that's **crowning**, and that's the "ring of fire."

**Tearing** means the tissue gives way rather than stretching. It's graded in four degrees:

| Degree | What it involves | How common |
|---|---|---|
| **1st** | Skin only | Common; heals quickly |
| **2nd** | Skin + perineal muscle (the most common tear) | Common; sutured with local anaesthetic |
| **3rd** | Extends into the anal sphincter complex | Uncommon |
| **4th** | Extends through the anal sphincter into the rectal lining | Rare, but the one worth preventing |

**The honest headline:** most first-time mothers have *some* graze or minor tear — that's normal and heals well. The goal of everything in this guide is not "no tear at all." It's stacked odds against a **severe (3rd/4th degree)** tear, and a better recovery if a minor one happens.

**Episiotomy** (a surgical cut) is no longer routine. The evidence settled this decades ago: routine episiotomy *increases* the risk of severe tears compared with letting the tissue stretch, without improving outcomes for mother or baby. It still has a place in genuine emergencies — shoulder dystocia, or a baby that needs to be out now — and that's it. Your birth preferences already say "no routine episiotomy" (see [`PROGRAM.md`](../../program/PROGRAM.md) template); the useful advancement on that is a question: *"What is your episiotomy rate, and do you support warm compresses and slow delivery of the head?"*

**Why "tight" is not the goal.** Here's the counter-intuitive bit, and it's the thesis of this whole week: **a strong pelvic floor and a tight pelvic floor are not the same thing.** Pregnancy and birth ask the pelvic floor to *lengthen and yield* — to let the baby past. A pelvic floor held in chronic tension (hypertonic, common in women who've been told to "do your Kegels" for months, or who hold tension in the jaw and pelvis as a stress pattern) is the one that tears or stalls. Hence the program's insistence, since Week 25, that the **release** matters more than the squeeze. Quick flicks this week exist to train the *release* half of the cycle, not to add more grip.

**How tissue elasticity actually works:** skin and fascia stretch best when they're well hydrated, well perfused, and used to being stretched. That's the mechanism behind all three of this week's tools — massage (mechanical stretching + desensitisation), nutrition (collagen and hydration), and daily movement (circulation). None is dramatic. Together they're the plan.

## 3. Perineal Massage — The Complete Protocol

This is the practice, start to finish. Read it once, then follow the print-out in Section 9 (or the version in [`physical.md`](physical.md)) — you don't need to memorise technique.

### When and how often

| Weeks | Frequency | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| **34–35** | 3–4× per week | 5 min per session |
| **36–37** | Daily | 5 min |
| **38 → birth** | Daily, if comfortable | 5 min |

**Start this week or next — whichever your provider says.** The Cochrane evidence applies to massage started from **34 weeks**, so there's no benefit to starting later, and nothing to be gained by forcing yourself if it's painful or you hate it. This is a preference, not a medical obligation.

### The technique, step by step

1. **Set up:** bladder empty, hands washed, nails short. Warm shower or a warm compress first helps the tissue relax. Oils: **water-soluble lubricant, sweet almond, coconut, vitamin E, or a dedicated perineal massage oil.** Avoid anything with fragrance, warming agents, or essential oils that sting. Don't use petroleum jelly (it doesn't absorb and traps bacteria).
2. **Get into position.** The easiest setup is **semi-reclined with knees bent and apart**, propped on pillows; or one foot up on the edge of the bath or a chair; or side-lying. A mirror helps with placement at first.
3. **Insert the thumbs** (or one thumb and one forefinger) **1–1.5 inches (2–4 cm) into the vagina**, pressing gently *downward* toward the rectum and *outward* toward the sides — this is the direction the head will travel, so it's the direction that stretches.
4. **Stretch in a U-shape.** Holding the pressure, sweep slowly from the 6 o'clock position (toward the anus) around to 3 o'clock and 9 o'clock, and back. That's one pass. **Hold each end of the U for 60–90 seconds**; the total session is 2–4 minutes of actual massage.
5. **Breathe and release while you do it.** This is the part that matters more than the technique: exhale, drop the jaw, and consciously let the pelvic floor soften *under* your thumbs. You're training a coordinated response — stretch + release — which is exactly what the second stage of labour asks for.
6. **Finish** by gently massaging the perineal skin and the outer tissue. Nothing else needed. Wash your hands.

### What it should feel like

**A stretching sensation, pressure, and a mild burning or tingling — that's the target.** **Sharp pain, stinging that lingers, or bleeding is not** — that means less pressure, more lubricant, or stop. Expect the first week to be awkward and a little uncomfortable; most women report it becomes routine within a week or two, and the local desensitisation is part of the point.

### The partner-assisted version

If reaching comfortably is difficult (which it often is at 34 weeks and beyond), the partner does it — and it's a genuinely useful rehearsal, because **he'll be applying perineal support and warm compresses on the day.** Walk him through it once this week, in daylight, calmly, so it's a normal clinic task rather than a first-time experiment in a delivery suite:

1. Same setup — she's semi-reclined with knees apart, pillows, a towel under her, warm compress applied first.
2. **He uses one or two lubricated fingers** (thumb is too blunt for a partner's angle), inserting 1–1.5 inches and pressing *downward toward the rectum*, not inward.
3. He should be **looking at her, not just the anatomy** — checking her jaw and breathing, and slowing down when she tenses. The instruction is the same as the massage: *"Exhale as I stretch."*
4. **She is in charge of pressure and pace** at all times, and says "more," "less," or "stop" without needing to explain.
5. He washes his hands before and after, and keeps nails short. Ten minutes of instruction covers it.

### When NOT to do perineal massage

| Do not massage | Why |
|---|---|
| **Placenta previa** (or any low-lying placenta your provider is monitoring) | Anything inserted near the cervix is off the table |
| **Active vaginal infection** (including thrush with symptoms) | Treat first, then start |
| **Active herpes outbreak** | Wait until it's fully healed, and talk to your provider |
| **Any unexplained vaginal bleeding or fluid leak** | Call your provider first — this is a red flag, not a massage question |
| **After your waters have broken** | No vaginal insertion of anything once labour has started |
| **If your provider has advised against it** | Their assessment of your specific anatomy wins over this document |

## 4. What the Evidence Actually Says (The Honest Version)

The relevant synthesis is the **Cochrane review of antenatal perineal massage** (Beckmann & Stock, 2013) — four trials, ~2,500 women, massage begun from 34 weeks:

- **Perineal trauma requiring stitches: reduced by roughly 9% in relative terms** (risk ratio ~0.91). On a baseline where most first-time mothers need some suturing, that's a modest absolute difference — roughly one woman in eleven who would otherwise have been stitched avoiding it.
- The benefit came mostly from **fewer 2nd-degree tears** and **fewer episiotomies**, and it was concentrated in **first-time mothers** and in those who did it **most days**.
- The pooled analysis **did not show a statistically significant reduction in 3rd/4th-degree tears.** So the strong, romantic claim — "perineal massage prevents severe tears" — is not what the pooled data supports.
- No evidence of harm in the trials. The main cost is the five minutes and the awkwardness.

**Be clear about the size of this:** it's real, it's free, and it's modest. It is one small lever among the several that matter more:

| Lever | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Warm compresses in the second stage** | Cochrane (Aasheim et al., 2017): probably reduces 3rd/4th-degree tears. Ask for them — they're in your birth preferences |
| **Slow, controlled birth of the head** | Letting the perineum stretch gradually rather than pushing the head out on a coached count. The single most protective thing in the room |
| **Not pushing on your back in stirrups** | Upright and side-lying positions use gravity and widen the outlet. Back-on-stirrups narrows it (Week 31) |
| **Spontaneous (open-glottis) pushing** | Follow your own urge rather than a 10-count breath-hold; less fatigue, no worse perineal outcomes, and easier on the baby |
| **Perineal massage from 34 weeks** | This week. Modest, real, optional |
| **Epi-No-type devices** | Trials so far have not shown a clear reduction in severe tears. Skip unless your provider specifically recommends it |

**So why bother?** Three reasons that survive the modest evidence. It's cheap and harmless. It gets you *familiar* with the sensation of the tissue stretching — so crowning isn't a first-time experience on the day. And it forces five minutes a session of practising release-under-stretch, which is the same skill as the pelvic floor breathing you've been doing since Week 30. Do it because the down-side is nil. Skip the guilt if you do skip it.

## 5. Tissue Health From the Inside: What You Eat and Drink

Elasticity is supported by the basics you've already got in the program, now with a specific purpose:

- **Protein 80–100 g/day** — collagen synthesis needs amino acids. Eggs, chicken, fish, tofu, lentils, yoghurt.
- **Vitamin C** — a required cofactor for collagen cross-linking. Citrus, capsicum, guava, kiwi, broccoli. Take iron with it (absorption, and iron needs peak now with blood volume at its maximum).
- **Hydration 3 L/day** — skin and fascia that are well hydrated stretch; dehydration is also the most common cause of cramping and irritable Braxton Hicks in Singapore's heat.
- **Collagen sources** — bone broth, or simply a varied protein intake. The evidence for collagen supplements specifically is thin; the evidence for adequate protein is not.
- **Electrolytes** — coconut water, banana, a pinch of sea salt, especially in the heat.
- **Small, frequent meals** — the stomach is compressed; five mini-meals beat three big ones, and the heartburn advice in [`THIRD-TRIMESTER-GUIDE.md`](../../program/THIRD-TRIMESTER-GUIDE.md) applies more every week.
- **Omega-3 (DHA)** — continue; it's on the prenatal list for the baby's brain as much as for you.

And the pelvic floor counterpart: **release work beats strengthening work** from here to birth. Cat-cow, deep squat, and the 3-minute pelvic floor release breathing are daily. A pelvic floor that can only grip is the risk factor; one that can lengthen on cue is the asset.

## 6. On the Day: How the Perineum Is Protected

Everything above is homework for one specific moment — the last few minutes of the second stage. The plan, in order:

1. **Positions that open the outlet:** supported squat, hands-and-knees, side-lying. Not flat on your back.
2. **Warm compresses** applied to the perineum by the midwife as the head descends. Ask for them by name; they're in the preferences document.
3. **Perineal support** with a controlled, slow birth of the head — the midwife's hands, or yours alone if you prefer.
4. **Slow down at crowning.** When the head is stretching the perineum, the instruction is to **stop pushing and breathe** — short panting breaths, jaw loose, letting the tissue do the last millimetre. The instinct to clamp down and finish is strong; resisting it for two or three contractions is what protects the tissue.
5. **Open-glottis pushing throughout** — sound out, no purple-faced breath-holding.
6. **A warm, upright, unhurried environment** — same as every other part of this program.

If a tear does happen, ask the midwife to grade it and explain the repair before it's done. Most 1st/2nd-degree tears heal uneventfully. If a 3rd/4th-degree tear is diagnosed, ask for a referral to a pelvic floor physiotherapist **before** discharge — early rehab matters, and it's the single best thing you can do for the next birth and for the rest of your life.

## 7. This Week's Appointment: The GBS Swab

**Group B Streptococcus (GBS)** is a common bacterium that lives harmlessly in the vagina or rectum of roughly **10–30% of women**. It causes no symptoms and is not a sexually transmitted infection, not a hygiene failure, and not a complication of pregnancy. It matters for exactly one reason: **a newborn who picks it up during birth can develop early-onset GBS disease** — a serious infection (sepsis, pneumonia, meningitis).

**The numbers, so this feels proportionate:**

| | |
|---|---|
| Colonised mothers | ~10–30% |
| Babies of colonised, *untreated* mothers who become unwell | ~1–2% |
| Risk reduction from IV antibiotics in labour | ~80% |
| Effect of being GBS-positive on how you give birth | None. It is not a reason for induction, and not a reason for cesarean |

**How the swab is taken:** a soft swab of the lower vagina and the rectum (a quick, painless touch-swab, done by your provider — you do not do it yourself). **Timing:** current CDC/ACOG guidance is **36w 0d – 37w 6d**, because a swab taken more than ~5 weeks before delivery is a less reliable predictor of colonisation at birth.

> ⚠️ **One discrepancy to resolve in pen this week:** your [`THIRD-TRIMESTER-GUIDE.md`](../../program/THIRD-TRIMESTER-GUIDE.md) appointment table schedules the GBS swab at this week's (34-week) visit, while [`PROGRAM.md`](../../program/PROGRAM.md) lists it at the 36-week appointment. Bring it up: *"If we swab at 34 weeks, will it be repeated closer to 36–37 weeks so the result is valid at delivery?"* Write the answer in the antenatal booklet so it isn't re-litigated at 2 a.m.

**If it comes back positive**, the plan changes in one specific, manageable way:

- **Intrapartum IV antibiotics** (usually penicillin, or clindamycin/vancomycin if allergic) once labour is established.
- **Antibiotics need about 4 hours before delivery to work properly** — which is exactly why **GBS-positive means you go in earlier**. Don't wait for 5-1-1: call when contractions are regular, when your waters break (immediately, day or night), or even when you're simply unsure. This is written into [`LABOUR-DAY-GUIDE.md`](../../program/LABOUR-DAY-GUIDE.md) §2.2 and §1.4 — the husband's copy. Make sure he's read it, and that your birth plan has a line for it.
- **Everything else stays the same:** mobile labour, movement, positions, shower, unmedicated plan, skin-to-skin, delayed clamping. A positive swab adds one drip and one earlier departure.
- **If it comes back negative**, nothing changes — proceed as planned. A negative swab is valid for about five weeks, which is another reason the timing question above is worth asking.

**Add to the one-page birth plan now:** *"GBS status: ____. If positive, IV antibiotics in labour — please note and start early; we will come in as soon as labour is suspected."*

## ⚠️ When to Call, Not Read

**Call TMC Maternity / your OB on-call immediately — day or night — for:** any regular contractions before 37 weeks · water breaking (note the time and colour) · bright red bleeding · reduced or changed fetal movement · severe headache with visual changes or upper-abdominal pain · fever · a gut feeling that something is wrong. A false alarm is the correct outcome of that call, every time.

And the specific one for this week: **do not start perineal massage through an infection, an outbreak, unexplained bleeding, or a fluid leak.** Call first, massage later.
