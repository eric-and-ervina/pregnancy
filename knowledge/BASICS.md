Here is a crash-course in the basics of labor and birth for you, the husband/partner. Think of this as your study guide: learn it, review it, and keep it handy.

**Important:** This is general education. Always follow your wife’s doctor, midwife, and hospital/birth center instructions.

---

## 1. Core Vocabulary You Need to Know

- **Contraction:** The uterus tightening and relaxing. It’s what opens the cervix.
- **Dilation:** How open the cervix is, measured in centimeters (0–10 cm). 10 cm = fully dilated.
- **Effacement:** How thin/short the cervix is, measured in percent (0–100%). First labors often efface a lot before dilating much; later labors tend to do both together.
- **Station:** How far the baby’s head has moved down into the pelvis, usually −5 (high/floating) to +5 (crowning), with 0 = at the ischial spines. (Some providers use −3 to +3 — same idea.) Negative = higher; positive = lower.
- **Membranes / “Water Breaking”:** The amniotic sac around the baby rupturing. It can be a gush or a slow leak. Note the time, color, and smell, and call your provider right away.
- **Bloody Show:** Pink or brown mucus plug. Small amount = labor may be near but not necessarily immediate. Bright-red heavy bleeding like a period is different — call immediately.
- **Meconium:** Baby’s first stool. If the fluid is green/brown, tell the provider — the team wants to know.
- **GBS (Group B Strep):** Bacteria screened for near term. If positive, mom needs antibiotics in labor, especially after water breaks.
- **Epidural:** Pain medication given through a small catheter in the lower back into the epidural space. It numbs the lower body. Requires IV, continuous monitoring, and usually a bladder catheter.
- **Induction:** Starting labor artificially, often with prostaglandins, Foley balloon, and/or Pitocin (synthetic oxytocin) drip.
- **Augmentation:** Speeding up labor that has already started (e.g., Pitocin, breaking water).
- **Fetal Monitoring:** Checking the baby’s heart rate. Can be external (belts) or internal (a small electrode on baby’s scalp after water breaks).
- **C-Section:** Surgical birth through incisions in the abdomen and uterus.
- **APGAR:** A quick 0–10 score given at 1 and 5 minutes after birth. Checks heart rate, breathing, muscle tone, reflex, and color. 7–10 is reassuring.
- **Placenta:** The organ that nourished the baby. It delivers after the baby, usually within 5–30 minutes.
- **Golden Hour:** The first hour after birth, often used for skin-to-skin contact and first breastfeeding attempt.
- **BRAIN:** Decision tool — Benefits, Risks, Alternatives, Intuition, Nothing / what if we wait. Use it when an intervention is offered.

## 2. How Labor Usually Starts

Labor can begin in several ways:

- **Regular contractions** that get stronger, longer, and closer together.
- **Water breaking** — a gush or trickle of fluid.
- **Bloody show** — pink or brown mucus, small amount.
- **Backache, cramps, or pelvic pressure.**
- Nesting urge / burst of energy can happen but is not a reliable medical sign.

**True vs. false labor:**

| False Labor | True Labor |
|---|---|
| Irregular contractions | Regular, timed contractions |
| Stop with rest, water, or position change | Continue and get stronger |
| Usually mild, felt mostly in front | Increase in intensity, often wraps front-to-back |
| No cervical change | Cervix dilates and effaces |

**How to time contractions:** From the *start* of one to the *start* of the next = frequency. How long each lasts = duration. Time for at least 1 hour.

**When to call the provider — don’t wait for a rule if any of these happen:**
- Contractions are **5-1-1** or **4-1-1**: every 5 or 4 minutes, lasting 1 minute, for 1 hour. Follow your provider’s specific rule.
- Water breaks — note time, color (clear vs. green/brown), and whether GBS-positive. Do not put anything in the vagina; call right away.
- Vaginal bleeding heavier than spotting, especially bright red.
- Baby is moving clearly less than usual — needs prompt evaluation.
- Severe constant pain, fever ≥38°C, severe headache, vision changes, sudden swelling, or fluid that is green/brown or foul-smelling.
- Before 37 weeks: regular contractions, fluid leak, bleeding, or constant pelvic pressure — call immediately, don’t wait for 5-1-1.

## 3. The Stages of Labor — What’s Happening and What You Do

Modern staging: first stage = latent + active (transition is the intense tail of active, not a separate medical stage), second stage = pushing, third = placenta, fourth = recovery.

| Stage | What’s Happening | What You Do |
|---|---|---|
| **First stage: Latent / Early** | Cervix opens ~0–6 cm. Contractions mild to moderate, may be irregular. Can take hours to a day+. | Keep her comfortable. Offer water, light snacks if allowed, rest, walking, shower. Time contractions. Don’t rush to hospital unless instructed — early arrival often slows labor. |
| **First stage: Active (incl. Transition 8–10 cm)** | Cervix ~6–10 cm. Contractions stronger, longer, closer. Transition is very intense — she may shake, vomit, feel hot/cold, or say “I can’t do this.” | Be her coach. Help with breathing, massage, counter-pressure, position changes, empty bladder every 1–2 h. Keep room dark, quiet, calm. Don’t take anger personally — “I can’t” often means transition and baby is close. |
| **Second stage: Pushing** | Cervix is 10 cm + urge to push / pressure. 30 min to 3+ h is normal (longer for first baby or with epidural). | Support her head, hold a leg if asked, count breaths if she wants, encourage between pushes. Follow the doctor/midwife’s cues. Upright / side-lying often helps if allowed. |
| **Third stage: Placenta** | Baby is born. Placenta delivers ~5–30 min later with mild contractions. | Hold the baby if she wants. Take photos. Help with skin-to-skin. Stay close. |
| **Fourth stage: Recovery** | First 1–2 h after birth. Uterus contracts, bleeding monitored, vitals checked. | Stay. Help with first feed, keep her warm, offer water. Watch with the nurse for heavy bleeding or dizziness. |

## 4. Pain Relief Options — The Basics

**Natural / Non-Medical:**
- Breathing techniques
- Massage and counter-pressure (hips, lower back)
- Position changes: walking, squatting, hands-and-knees, side-lying, birth ball / peanut ball
- Warm shower or bath (if allowed)
- Heat/cold packs
- Music, dim lights, quiet room, privacy

**Medical:**
- **IV pain meds (opioids):** Take the edge off but don’t fully remove pain. Can make mom sleepy/nauseous and baby drowsy — timing matters near birth.
- **Nitrous oxide:** Self-inhaled gas. Helps with anxiety/pain; wears off quickly. Doesn’t remove pain. Not offered everywhere — ask your hospital in advance.
- **Epidural:** Most effective. Numbs lower body but requires IV, continuous fetal/BP monitoring, limited mobility, and usually a bladder catheter. Can lower blood pressure, cause itching/fever/urinary retention, and may lengthen pushing. Serious headache from dural puncture is uncommon (~1%). Modern evidence does not show it necessarily raises C-section rate.

**Your job:** Know her preferences. If she changes her mind, support her. Don’t guilt her. The goal is a safe mom and baby.

## 5. Common Interventions You May Hear About

- **IV fluids:** For hydration and medication access.
- **Continuous monitoring:** Belts or internal monitor to watch baby’s heart rate.
- **GBS antibiotics:** IV antibiotics in labor if GBS-positive.
- **Induction / augmentation:** Prostaglandins, Foley balloon, Pitocin drip to start or strengthen contractions.
- **Artificial rupture of membranes:** Doctor/midwife breaks the water to speed labor — ask about timing and GBS status.
- **Internal monitor:** A small clip on the baby’s scalp for accurate heart rate (only after water breaks).
- **Bladder catheter:** Often placed with an epidural so she doesn’t have to get up to pee. Otherwise remind her to pee every 1–2 h — a full bladder blocks descent.
- **Vacuum or forceps:** Help guide the baby out if pushing is stalled and conditions are right.
- **Episiotomy:** A cut to enlarge the vaginal opening. Now rare; usually only if urgent. Tears can still happen and may need stitches.
- **C-Section:** If baby or mom is in distress, labor stalls despite measures, baby is malpositioned, or baby can’t fit through the pelvis.

**Your job:** Ask with BRAIN: Benefits? Risks? Alternatives? What does she feel? What if we wait? You are her advocate, not her doctor.

## 6. Your Role — The Basics

- **Be calm.** Your calm helps her stay calm. Low voice, slow breathing.
- **Be her voice.** She may be focused inward. Speak up if something doesn’t match her birth plan.
- **Be physical.** Hold her hand, rub her back, wipe her face, offer water/straw, lip balm.
- **Be patient.** Labor is unpredictable. First labors average 12–18 h. Rest when she rests.
- **Be humble.** If she wants her mom, a doula, or silence instead of you, that’s okay.
- **Be fed and rested.** You cannot support her if you collapse. Eat, drink, sleep when you can.
- **Be the gatekeeper.** Limit visitors, chatter, and bright lights. Protect her rest and privacy.

## 7. After Birth — The Basics

- **Golden Hour:** Skin-to-skin with mom (or you if mom can’t). Delayed cord clamping if desired/possible. First breastfeeding attempt.
- **Placenta delivery:** Mild contractions. She may shake or feel cold — normal.
- **Fundal massage:** Nurse presses on her belly to help the uterus contract and prevent bleeding. It can be painful — warn her, hold her hand.
- **Stitches:** If she tore or had an episiotomy, she may need repair — ask about degree and care.
- **First pee / standing:** She may need help to the bathroom. Dizziness is common. Go with her.
- **Bleeding (lochia):** Heavy period-like bleeding for days to weeks. Call for: soaking ≥1 pad/hour for 2+ hours, clots larger than a golf ball, foul-smelling discharge, fever, worsening pain, or heavy bleeding that returns after easing.
- **Breastfeeding:** Can be hard. Help with pillows, water, snacks, and encouragement. Ask for the lactation nurse early.
- **Emotions:** Baby blues (tearful, 3–5 days, eases by ~2 weeks) are common. Postpartum depression/anxiety can affect both moms and dads. Get help for persistent sadness, anger, irritability, feeling trapped, panic, or trouble bonding.

**Call urgently after discharge for:** heavy bleeding, severe headache/vision changes, sudden swelling, fever, chest pain/trouble breathing, calf pain/swelling, wound redness/drainage, inability to pee, or thoughts of harming self — for either parent.

**Your job after birth:**
- Take over cooking, cleaning, laundry, and diaper changes.
- Bring the baby to her for feeds.
- Protect her sleep — visitors on her schedule, not yours.
- Watch for signs of postpartum depression in both of you.
- Bond with your baby: hold, burp, soothe, skin-to-skin.

- [ ] Know the provider’s phone number, after-hours line, and hospital route.
- [ ] Know her birth plan and preferences, incl. GBS status and epidural preference.
- [ ] Pack your own hospital bag + document folder.
- [ ] Install the car seat.
- [ ] Know the 5-1-1 or 4-1-1 rule + how to time contractions.
- [ ] Know what to do if water breaks (time/color/call, nothing in vagina).
- [ ] Know pain relief options and what your hospital actually offers.
- [ ] Know common interventions + BRAIN questions.
- [ ] Know your role: advocate, coach, calm presence.
- [ ] Plan postpartum help: meals, cleaning, visitors.
- [ ] Know urgent warning signs in labor and postpartum, and signs of postpartum depression.

---

## 9. Five Things to Memorize

1. **Labor is unpredictable.** Plans change. Stay flexible.
2. **Your calm is contagious.** Breathe. She will follow.
3. **She needs you to be her voice.** Ask BRAIN questions. Speak up.
4. **“I can’t do this” often means transition.** It’s a sign of progress, not failure.
5. **After birth, recovery matters.** Your job shifts from coach to protector and partner.

If you learn these basics, you’ll be far more prepared than most. Review them with your wife, ask her what she wants, and write down your own notes. You’ve got this.
