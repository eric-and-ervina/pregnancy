# Chapter 4 — The First 48 Hours

> Read this in ~12 minutes. The golden hour, the newborn procedures, and your job list for the first two days. This chapter is education, not medical advice — the midwife standing next to you has the final say.

---

## The golden hour, condensed

The first hour after birth is the most important hour of the week. Three things matter here. Everything else can wait.

**1. Skin-to-skin, immediately.** As soon as he's out and breathing fine, the midwife places him bare-chest on Ervina's chest, under a warm blanket. Don't dress him first, don't take him away for photos. He regulates his breathing, heart rate and temperature off her body better than any warmer. Your job: help position him so her hands are free, then *leave them alone*. Quiet room. No crowd.

**2. Delayed cord clamping.** Unless there's a reason to rush (rare), the cord stays attached for 1–3 minutes while it keeps pulsing blood into him. This is standard practice at Singapore hospitals now and it's in your birth plan — but say it out loud anyway, because people forget in the moment. If you've already discussed it with your OBGYN, a simple "We're still on for delayed clamping, right?" is enough.

**3. The first latch, within the hour.** The first feed goes best in the first hour — he's alert, then he'll crash into a long sleep. Ask the midwife: "Can you help her with the first latch now?" That one sentence matters. Say it early, before the alert window closes.

**What you insist on:** the three things above, plus a quiet room — you are the gatekeeper. One-line replies to family messages, phone face-down, nobody rushing her.

**What can wait:** his first full bath (nurses often delay it; it doesn't need to happen in the first hour), detailed paperwork, naming him, the "official" photos. None of that is urgent.

For the full nine-stage breakdown of what the baby actually does in that first hour — and why interrupted skin-to-skin causes real problems — read [../weeks/week-32/reading.md](../weeks/week-32/reading.md), which goes deep on the golden hour.

## Newborn procedures at the hospital: what happens and why

These happen quickly, mostly in the delivery suite or the nursery, while you're staying close. You'll be allowed to watch most of it. Here's what's coming so nothing surprises you:

- **Drying and warming.** Immediate. A warm towel, vigorous drying, warm blanket. Keeps his temperature stable.
- **APGAR score (1, 5 and 10 minutes).** This is not a grade and it is not a diagnosis — it's a quick snapshot of how he's transitioning to life outside, scored 0–2 on five things: **A**ppearance (skin colour), **P**ulse (heart rate), **G**rimace (reflexes), **A**ctivity (muscle tone), **R**espiration (breathing). Out of 10. Scores of 7–10 are normal. It's done three times because the score often rises as he adjusts. A slightly low score at 1 minute that recovers by 5 is common and usually means nothing — don't panic at a single number.
- **Vitamin K injection.** One small shot into his thigh. This is the standard of care everywhere and it prevents a rare but dangerous bleeding disorder (vitamin K deficiency bleeding) that breastfed babies are at risk of because breast milk is naturally low in vitamin K. Not optional in practice — the paediatrician will recommend it strongly.
- **Eye ointment (erythromycin).** Prevents eye infection. Routine.
- **Weighing and measuring.** Length, head circumference, weight. The weight number becomes your reference point — newborns typically lose some weight in the first days (more on that in chapter 5).
- **BCG + Hepatitis B dose 1.** Given in hospital before discharge under Singapore's National Childhood Immunisation Schedule (NCIS), and confirmed included in TMC's maternity packages. The next shots: **Hep B dose 2 at 1 month**, **6-in-1 dose 1 at 2 months**. Write these into your calendar before you leave the ward.

The hearing screen and the full physical exam by the paediatrician also happen during the stay — ask the nurse what each check is for as they do it. You are allowed to be curious. It makes you calmer, and calm parents learn faster.

## Your jobs in the first 48 hours

Here's the truth nobody prints on the brochure: for the first two days, you are the project manager of a very small, very important operation. She's recovering. He's figuring out feeding. You handle everything else.

**1. Paperwork and admin.** Your most valuable skill this week is not baby-holding, it's admin. Keep the folder: discharge summary, medication list, the ward's 24-hour number (take this — you *will* have a question at 3am), the paediatrician follow-up appointment details, vaccination record book. Take photos of every document the moment you receive it. The birth registration chain (ICA → Indonesian embassy passport → baby's pass, all within 42 days) starts now — see [../program/LABOUR-DAY-GUIDE.md](../program/LABOUR-DAY-GUIDE.md) PART 8.2 for the full sequence. Don't try to finish it all in the ward; just start the clock.

**2. Be the gatekeeper.** The ward is not a receiving line. Limit visitors, keep visits short, and send people home when she's tired. If someone offers "help," the helpful version is food at your HDB door and no visit. You say this so she doesn't have to.

**3. Ask the nurses to teach you — this is your school.** The single best use of your time in the ward is learning from the people who do this all day. Ask for a demo of everything:
- How to change a diaper (meconium is sticky and black — you'll see it on day 1; normal)
- How to swaddle
- How to hold him for a feed and how to burp him
- How to sponge-bathe him
- How to soothe him when he cries and you're the only one there

They are genuinely happy to teach. They'd rather show you than redo it. Ask twice if you forget.

**4. Dad skin-to-skin.** If she's getting stitched up, or is too exhausted for skin-to-skin, the chest is yours. Unbutton your shirt, baby bare-chest against your skin, blanket over both. This is not a consolation prize — it does the same temperature and heart-rate regulation work, and it keeps him with a parent instead of in a bassinet. It also calms *you* down.

**5. Ward stay basics.** For an uncomplicated natural birth, expect **about 2 days** at TMC (confirm against your package). If the baby stays with her — rooming-in — **you two are the night shift.** The nurses are there for medical things, not for holding the baby all night. Sleep in shifts: the awake one handles the baby, the other sleeps properly. Tag-team; don't both stay awake. The full ward logistics (your overnight arrangements, food, parking, charging) are in [PART 8.1 and 8.4 of the Labour Day Guide](../program/LABOUR-DAY-GUIDE.md) — skim them once more this week.

**6. Watch for jaundice — the usual reason discharge slips.** Many babies look a little yellow on day 2–3. The nurses check with a meter on the skin or a blood test. Most mild jaundice clears on its own; sometimes they want to keep him another day under lights (phototherapy) or re-test after you go home. If discharge is delayed, it is almost always this, and it is almost always fine. Chapter 5 covers what to watch for once you're home.

**7. Photos and the one announcement.** Take the first-day photos (they're for her, mostly — she'll want them even if she feels terrible). One announcement message to both families, with name, weight, time, and "mother and baby well." Then put the phone down.

## The discharge checklist

Before you walk out of TMC, you have:

- [ ] Discharge summary — read it, don't just file it
- [ ] Medication list (hers and baby's), with how and when for each
- [ ] Ward's 24-hour number saved in your phone
- [ ] Paediatrician follow-up appointment booked (usually within a few days; jaundice re-check is often the reason)
- [ ] Vaccination record book / NCIS schedule noted (Hep B dose 2 at 1 month)
- [ ] Car seat fitted — harness straps snug, recline angle right. Do this *before* the day if possible; if not, do it in the carpark without rushing
- [ ] Her discharge paperwork (stitches care instructions, pain medication, her 6-week check appointment)
- [ ] Your phone charged, her bag packed, the folder in one place

## What to expect from her physically (the short version)

Chapter 7 goes deep on her recovery. For these 48 hours, here's what's normal:

- **Vaginal soreness and bleeding (lochia).** Expect bleeding like a heavy period, gradually tapering over weeks. Soaking a pad in an hour or less is *not* normal — that's a call the ward / 995 situation.
- **Afterpains.** Cramping as the uterus shrinks, often worse during feeds (oxytocin). Normal.
- **Shaky first stands.** She'll feel wobbly the first time she gets up. Take her arm, walk slowly, follow the nurse's guidance. Support her mobility — she should not be carrying the laundry or bending much.
- **Exhaustion deeper than tired.** She has just done the hardest physical work of her life, on adrenaline, with broken sleep. Your job is to make sure she does *nothing* else. She lies down, feeds, sleeps. That's the list.
- **Breasts and early feeds.** Colostrum (the first milk) comes in tiny amounts — teaspoons, not ounces. That is normal and it is enough. Her milk "coming in" happens around day 2–4 and can be uncomfortable. Full detail in chapter 6.

Your line for anyone who visits: *"She's resting. Short visit."* Then enforce it.

## Costs: what to have in your head

TMC's normal vaginal 2-day package (official July-2024 sheet, **hospital charges only** — confirm 2026 prices against your written estimate): 4-bedded **S$2,960.44**, 2-bedded **S$3,265.64**, 1-bedded **S$4,152.90**, Premier single **S$4,986.75**, suites **S$6,828.85–S$10,098.85**. Epidural, induction drugs, a longer stay, or special-care nursery time each add on top. Get the estimated bill in writing from TMC Pre-admission *before* the day and know what deposit they require at admission — see [PART 9 of the Labour Day Guide](../program/LABOUR-DAY-GUIDE.md) for the full money-and-leave briefing.
