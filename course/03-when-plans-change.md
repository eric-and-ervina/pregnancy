# Chapter 3 — When Plans Change

*Read this in about 15 minutes. Read it NOW, while you're calm — not when someone in scrubs is asking you to sign a consent form.*

> This chapter is education, not medical advice. Interventions are medical decisions — your gynae/midwife advises, you and your wife decide. Anything marked **[confirm with TMC/your gynae]** needs checking at the next appointment.

---

## Why this chapter exists

Most births deviate from the plan somewhere. That doesn't mean something went wrong — it means birth is a biological event, not a project timeline. This chapter is your pre-read so that **nothing on the day is the first time you're hearing about it.** When you've already read what a balloon catheter is at 10pm on a calm Monday, you can make a clear decision about one at 3am on a hard Friday.

The closing line of this chapter is the whole point: **the goal is a healthy mom and a healthy baby.** Everything else is preference.

---

## 1. Induction — when labour needs a start date

**Why it's offered:** going past 41 weeks (CASE ⑥), waters broken for a long time with no labour starting, or medical reasons (blood pressure, growth concerns, diabetes, etc.). Induction is *starting* labour artificially.

**The methods, in the order they're usually tried** — and what each means for your day:

- **Membrane sweep ("stretch and sweep").** Done in clinic, often from 39–40 weeks. The gynae sweeps a finger around the cervix to separate the membranes. Uncomfortable, spotting afterward is normal, and it only works if the body is nearly ready. Logistically: a clinic appointment, then go home and wait.
- **Balloon catheter (Foley bulb).** A small balloon placed through the cervix and inflated to mechanically open it. Usually done in hospital; takes hours; she can often walk around with it in. Mild cramping is normal.
- **Prostaglandin (gel/pessary).** Medication placed near the cervix to soften and thin it ("ripening"). Hospital stay, monitoring, waiting — often overnight.
- **Pitocin/Syntocinon drip (oxytocin IV).** Once the cervix is favourable, synthetic oxytocin starts contractions through a drip. This means: continuous monitoring, IV pole, mostly in bed. Contractions from Pitocin are often described as more intense with less rest between — which is why an epidural is commonly discussed alongside it (see below).

**Your job:** Ask BRAIN questions (Chapter 2). Key ones for induction: *Why now — what changes if we wait 24–48 hours? Which method are we starting with, and what's the next step if it doesn't work?* Induction can be a slow process — bring patience, snacks, and a charger. The full case detail is CASE ⑩ in ../program/LABOUR-DAY-GUIDE.md.

## 2. Augmentation — when labour needs a nudge

Labour started on its own but is long, slow, or stalled (CASE ⑪). The usual tools: breaking the waters artificially (amniotomy — quick, feels like a strong internal exam, often intensifies contractions fast) and/or a Pitocin drip.

**What to know:** "Failure to progress" is a diagnosis with a clock attached — ask what the clock is. Often the first moves are non-medical: position changes, walking, emptying the bladder, rest. Your advocacy line: *"Before augmentation — can we try [position change / an hour more] first?"* If augmentation is advised, run BRAIN and ask about pain-relief options *with* the Pitocin, since the combination is the classic setup where an epidural gets discussed.

## 3. Epidural — the honest guide

**What it is:** An anaesthetist places a thin catheter in the lower back; local anaesthetic numbs from roughly the waist down. She stays awake and alert. It can be topped up through labour.

**When it can be given:** Usually once labour is established (active labour) — too early can slow things; in transition it's often too late to take full effect. The anaesthetist and midwife advise on timing.

**Honest trade-offs:**
- Excellent pain relief for most women — the reason it exists.
- She'll likely be confined to bed with continuous monitoring and a bladder catheter; pushing may take longer and she may not feel the urge to push (coached pushing instead).
- Slightly higher chance of assisted delivery (vacuum/forceps) and of Pitocin augmentation, because numbed pushing is less effective.
- Rare risks (headache from dural puncture, blood pressure drop) — the anaesthetist covers these in consent; ask questions then.
- **Cost:** roughly **S$390–490** top-up at TMC depending on room class (from the Jul-2024 sheet — confirm 2026 prices with TMC).

**How to support her if she asks for it — the only script you need:**

She asks once, clearly → you say **"Okay. I'll get the midwife."** No "are you sure?", no "but the plan", no disappointed face. Zero guilt. **It is her call, full stop.** Your unmedicated plan was a preference; her real-time decision about her body outranks it completely.

Afterward: she may feel she "failed." She didn't. Tell her so, plainly, more than once — including in the weeks after. A rested mother who enjoyed meeting her baby beats an exhausted one who white-knuckled it for pride.

## 4. Assisted delivery — vacuum / forceps (brief)

If pushing has gone on a long time or the baby needs to come out faster, the doctor may suggest a ventouse (suction cup on the head) or forceps. It usually requires an episiotomy discussion and good pain relief (often an epidural top-up). It's quick when it works; if it doesn't, the next step is a c-section. Your job: stay at her head, explain nothing (the doctor will), hold her hand, and be the calm face she looks at. Details in CASE ⑫.

## 5. C-section — planned or emergency

**The difference that matters to you:** a planned/scheduled section is calm, daylight, playlist-optional. An emergency section is fast — and "emergency" covers a wide range from "we need to do this in the next hour" to "right now." Ask which one it is: *"Is this an emergency, or do we have 10 minutes?"*

**Step by step, what happens:**
1. Consent signed, she changes, IV and monitoring in.
2. Spinal anaesthesia — numb from the chest down, awake throughout. (General anaesthetic is rare and only for true crash emergencies.)
3. You scrub in: gown, cap, mask. You sit **beside her head**, behind the screen. Hold her hand, talk to her — she can't see what's happening and your voice is her whole world for the next 20 minutes.
4. Pressure and tugging sensations are normal; sharp pain is not — she should say so immediately.
5. Baby out — often within 10–15 minutes of starting. If all is well: **skin-to-skin in the operating theatre is often possible** — ask for it in advance [confirm with TMC/your gynae]. You may be the one holding him skin-to-skin while she's being stitched.
6. Stitching takes longer than the delivery (~30–45 min). Then recovery ward: shivering and nausea are common and pass.

**Recovery basics:** It's major abdominal surgery. First 24 hours: bed rest-ish, catheter, pain meds on schedule (take them — don't be heroic). First 2 weeks: no lifting beyond the baby, no driving [confirm with TMC/your gynae on timelines], accept all help offered. Full recovery ~6 weeks. If your wife ends up here, your workload roughly doubles — plan for it now (who's cooking? who's on night duty with you? your parents arriving from Jakarta will matter here).

**Your mindset:** A c-section is not the opposite of a good birth. It is a birth, performed by skilled people, that ends with your son in your arms. CASE ⑦ in ../program/LABOUR-DAY-GUIDE.md has the full version.

## 6. NICU / special care — brief

Sometimes the baby needs monitoring or support after birth — breathing help, jaundice treatment under lights, blood sugar monitoring. It's more common than people think and most NICU stays are short and boring (which is exactly what you want).

**What to know in advance:** You can usually visit, touch, and often do skin-to-skin even in NICU — ask the nurses what's possible. If he's there, your wife needs you shuttling between her recovery and him, plus someone needs to eat and sleep. This is when the grandparents-from-Jakarta plan earns its keep. Full detail in CASE ⑬.

## 7. Making decisions under pressure — the 60-second version

Tape this to the inside of your eyelids:

1. **One breath.** Slow down your own nervous system first.
2. **"Is this an emergency, or do we have 10 minutes?"** — Emergency: say yes, move. Not an emergency: you have time.
3. **BRAIN out loud** (Benefits, Risks, Alternatives, Intuition, Nothing — Chapter 2).
4. **Check with her.** Even a nod. It's her body.
5. **Decide, then commit.** No relitigating after. Second-guessing a made decision helps no one.

The only wrong move is freezing. A clear decision you both own beats a perfect decision made too late.

## 8. After the birth — the handoff to fatherhood

This chapter's job ends at birth; yours expands. Three things worth knowing now so November doesn't ambush you:

- **The ward stay** (PART 8 of ../program/LABOUR-DAY-GUIDE.md): ~2 days for vaginal, ~3–4 for c-section. Baby registration via LifeSG within 42 days (Chapter 1). Jaundice checks may delay discharge — normal, expected.
- **Feeding:** you and your wife are still deciding between full/partial/mixed breastfeeding. No position from this course — it's your family's call with your paediatrician. What's useful to know now: whatever you choose, the first week's job is *supporting the feeder* (water, food, company at 3am), not having opinions about the method.
- **Your repos have you covered from here:** the confinement meal plan lives in `eric-and-ervina/confinement`, the baby gear checklist in `eric-and-ervina/raising-child`. This course deliberately doesn't duplicate them — go read them next.

## 5b. Consent forms — read before you sign

You'll be handed consent forms for epidurals, c-sections, and procedures. In the moment, read the **three lines that matter**: what is being done, the main risks listed, and that you can ask questions first. You are allowed to say *"Give us two minutes to read this properly"* — even in a hurry, two minutes exists unless someone is shouting. Signing while understanding beats signing while panicking. If your wife can't meaningfully consent (exhausted, medicated), that's when your prior conversations about her preferences do the work — another reason to have talked through this chapter together *before* the day.

---

## 7b. The "what if it all happens at once" scenario

Worst realistic case, so it's not a surprise: long labour → augmentation → epidural → stalled pushing → assisted delivery → c-section → baby to NICU for observation. Every step of that chain is in this chapter and the linked cases. If it happens, run the same playbook at each step: one breath, "emergency or 10 minutes?", BRAIN, check with her, decide, commit. You don't need a new skill for each escalation — you need the same calm procedure, repeated.
