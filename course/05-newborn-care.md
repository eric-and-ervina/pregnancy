# Chapter 5 — The Newborn Manual

> Read this in ~20 minutes. Holding, diapering, bathing, swaddling, sleep, soothing, and what "normal" looks like in the first weeks. Education, not medical advice — your paediatrician's guidance overrides anything here.

---

## Holding him

The one rule: **always support his head and neck.** Newborns can't hold their heads up; the neck muscles need weeks. One hand cradles the head and neck, the other supports the bottom or back. When you pick him up, slide one hand under his head first, then the other under his bottom, then lift. When you put him down, bottom first, then ease the head down, then slide your hand out.

Three holds you'll actually use:

- **The cradle.** His head in the crook of your elbow, your forearm along his back, other hand under his bottom. The classic.
- **The football (upright on your shoulder).** Chest against yours, one hand behind his head and neck, the other supporting his bottom. Good for burping and for calming — and good for you, because it's less tiring.
- **The lap hold.** He lies across your forearm on his tummy, head turned to the side, while you sit. Great for fussy periods and colic-ish evenings. **Never for sleep** — this is a calming hold only, while you're awake.

You'll feel clumsy the first dozen times. That's normal. He won't break. Gentle, confident hands — hesitation is scarier for both of you than competence you don't feel yet.

## Diapering: the step-by-step

You will change roughly **8–12 diapers a day** in the first weeks. By day 5 you'll do it half-asleep. Here's the sequence the nurses will teach you in the ward — ask them to watch you do one:

1. Wash your hands (or sanitise).
2. Lay him on a clean, flat surface. **Never leave him unattended on a raised surface** — not for a second. He can't roll yet, but start the habit now.
3. Undo the dirty diaper. If it's poopy, use the front of the diaper itself to wipe the bulk off first.
4. Lift his legs gently by the ankles — or slide one hand under his bottom to lift. **Wipe front to back** (away from the genitals, toward the bottom). This prevents urinary tract infections. Fresh wipe for each pass.
5. Pat dry. Moisture is the enemy — most diaper rash is just wetness sitting too long.
6. Slide a clean diaper under him: the back edge at about his belly button level. The front comes up between his legs. Fasten the tabs snug enough to fit one finger underneath. The leg cuffs should be flared *out*, not tucked in.
7. **Fold the front of the diaper down below the cord stump** until the stump falls off (see cord care below).
8. Wash your hands again.

**For boys specifically:** wipe around (not under) the foreskin — don't retract it, just clean the outside. And keep a spare cloth over his front while you change him, because boy babies have legendary timing.

### What comes out of him, in order

- **Days 1–3: meconium.** Black, tarry, sticky. It's the amniotic fluid and mucus he's been swallowing in the womb. Hard to wipe off — coconut oil or a little petroleum jelly on the wipe helps.
- **Days 3–5: transitional stools.** Greenish-brown, looser. Means milk is coming through.
- **Day 5+: milk stools.** Yellow, seedy, loose — breastfed babies make the classic "mustard" stools. Formula-fed stools are paler, firmer, more pungent.

### Diaper counts: your feeding gauge

Nobody can see inside his stomach, so diapers are the readout. Rough guide (days counted from birth):

| Day | Wet diapers | Dirty diapers |
|-----|-------------|---------------|
| 1   | 1–2         | 1 (meconium)  |
| 2   | 2–3         | 2             |
| 3   | 3–4         | 2–3           |
| 4   | 4–5         | 3–4           |
| 5+  | 6+ (pale yellow pee) | 3+ |

By day 5, **6+ wet diapers in 24 hours** is the main sign he's getting enough milk. Write the counts down — an app or a notebook by the changing station. When the paediatrician asks "how's feeding going?" this is your evidence.

**Rash prevention:** change promptly (don't let him marinate), pat dry, give him 10–15 minutes of air-dry time on a towel when you can, and use a thin barrier cream (zinc oxide) at the first sign of redness. Persistent bright-red rash, blisters, or rash that spreads despite a day of barrier care — mention it to the paediatrician.

## Cord stump care

The umbilical stump is a small, dark, slightly alien-looking nub. It dries up and falls off in **1–3 weeks**. Until then:

- **Keep it clean and dry.** That's the whole job.
- Fold the diaper front *below* the stump so pee doesn't wick up into it.
- If it gets dirty, dab with clean water and pat dry. No alcohol unless your paediatrician says so — drying it out with alcohol is no longer the default advice in most places.
- Don't pull it, even if it's hanging by a thread. Let it drop on its own.
- A few drops of dried blood around the base when it falls off is normal.

**Call the doctor if:** the skin around it turns red or swollen, there's yellow/green pus, it smells foul, or it bleeds more than a spot. Cord infection is uncommon but real — don't wait on this one.

## Bathing

**Until the cord stump falls off: sponge baths only.** No tub, no submersion.

### Sponge bath how-to (2–3 times a week is plenty)

1. Warm room. Everything laid out beforehand: towel, clean diaper, clothes, cotton balls, a bowl of warm water.
2. Keep him wrapped in a towel and uncover one part at a time — newborns lose heat fast.
3. Plain warm water on a soft cloth for the face (no soap on the face). Separate cotton balls for each eye, wiping from the inner corner out.
4. Soap the body gently — mild, fragrance-free baby soap. Clean the neck folds, armpits, behind the ears, and the diaper area last.
5. Pat dry thoroughly, especially skin folds. Diaper, dress, done. The whole thing takes 5 minutes.

### After the cord falls: tub baths

Same frequency is fine — daily is not required, and too much bathing dries the skin.

1. Fill the tub/basin with **2–3 inches of warm water**. Test with your elbow or the inside of your wrist — it should feel warm, not hot. (If you want a number: around 37–38°C. Elbow-test is honestly more reliable than a thermometer once you practise.)
2. One hand always on him. **Never leave him unattended in water, not for a second** — not to answer the door, not to grab the towel you forgot. Drowning is silent and fast. Everything within arm's reach before he goes in.
3. Support his head above water with one hand, wash with the other. Start at the face, work down, diaper area last.
4. Lift him out onto a warm towel, pat dry (rubbing irritates newborn skin), diaper and dress quickly.

Most babies either love baths or hate them at first. If he screams through every bath for the first weeks, that's temperament, not a problem. Keep them short and warm.

## Swaddling

A snug swaddle calms the startle reflex and helps him sleep. The nurses will demo this in the ward — have them watch *you* do it, because you'll be doing it at 3am with one eye open.

The steps: lay a thin blanket in a diamond, fold the top corner down, lay him with shoulders at the fold, bring one side across his chest and tuck under his body, fold the bottom up (leave room for his legs — **hips should be able to move**, straight-jacket legs are bad for hip joints), bring the other side across and tuck.

Two rules that matter:

- **Snug around the chest, loose around the hips.** Tight swaddles that pin the legs straight are linked to hip dysplasia. His legs should be able to bend up and out.
- **Stop swaddling when he shows signs of rolling** — usually around 2–4 months. Rolling in a swaddle is a suffocation risk. Transition to a sleep sack then.

If he hates the swaddle and fights it every time, that's fine — some babies do. Try one arm out, or skip it. The swaddle serves the baby, not the other way round.

## Dressing and nails

**Dressing:** one more layer than you're comfortable in is the usual guide. Check the back of his neck — warm and dry is right; sweaty means too many layers, cool means add one. Avoid loose strings, cords, or anything around the neck. Hats indoors aren't needed once he's home and warm; the "babies lose heat through their heads" thing is overstated — they lose heat through everything, which is why the room and layers matter.

**Nails:** newborn nails are soft, thin, and surprisingly sharp — he will scratch his own face. **File, don't cut**, in the early weeks (a soft emery board). File while he sleeps or right after a feed. Tiny mittens work as a stopgap but don't rely on them long-term; he needs his hands free to explore.

## Safe sleep — the non-negotiables

This is the section with the least room for improvisation. Sudden infant death (SIDS) risk is reduced dramatically by a boring, consistent sleep setup:

- **Back to sleep, every sleep.** Naps and nights. No exceptions.
- **Bare sleep surface.** Firm, flat mattress in his own cot/bassinet. No pillows, no blankets, no toys, no bumpers, no positioners. A fitted sheet and a sleep sack are all he needs. If you're worried he's cold, add a layer of clothing or a warmer sleep sack — not a blanket.
- **Room-share, not bed-share.** He sleeps in your room for the first **6–12 months** — this alone significantly reduces SIDS risk. He does *not* sleep in your bed, on the sofa with you, or in an armchair with you. Sofa-sleeping with a baby is one of the highest-risk situations there is. If you're so exhausted you might fall asleep holding him, put him in the cot first — a crying baby in a cot is safe; a sleeping baby in your arms on a sofa is not.
- **Comfortable room temperature.** If the room is comfortable for a lightly-dressed adult, it's fine. Overheating is a risk factor — no hats indoors, no heavy blankets, and check the back of his neck for sweat.
- **Smoke-free.** No smoking around him, ever. If any visitor smokes, they wash hands and change their shirt before holding him.

Tell both sets of grandparents these rules before they arrive. Older advice was different, and "we did it this way and you survived" is not a safety argument.

## Soothing: the 5 S's

When he cries and it's not hunger, a wet diaper, or something medical, run this sequence:

1. **Swaddle** — snug, if he's under rolling age.
2. **Side or stomach hold** — hold him on his side or tummy-down on your forearm *while you are awake and holding him*. **Never** place him on his side or tummy to sleep.
3. **Shush** — loud, continuous white noise near his ear. As loud as his crying. A shushing app or a white-noise machine works.
4. **Swing** — rhythmic jiggling with tiny movements, supporting his head. Think "shivering," not rocking-chair. A baby swing or bouncer can help once he's calm-ish.
5. **Suck** — a clean finger, or a pacifier if you're using one. (If breastfeeding is being established, most lactation advice says wait 3–4 weeks before introducing a pacifier — [confirm with TMC/your paediatrician].)

Cycle through them. Combine them. Walk the corridor. Bounce on an exercise ball if you have one.

### When nothing works

Some evenings he will cry for hours and nothing on the list helps. This peaks around 6–8 weeks and it is miserable and it is normal. Two things:

1. **Hand over.** If there's another adult in the house, you are allowed — obligated, really — to pass him over when you're fraying. Tag-team in 20-minute blocks.
2. **Put him down safely and take 5 minutes.** A crying baby alone in a cot is safe. A parent who is shaking with frustration holding a baby is not. Put him on his back in the cot, walk out, breathe, drink water, come back. This is not neglect. This is the correct procedure.

**Never shake him.** Shaking causes brain injury that can kill. If you ever feel the impulse, put him down and walk away. Every time.

## Feeding cues — catch them early

Crying is the *last* signal, not the first. By the time he's crying, he's already upset and harder to settle at the breast or bottle. Learn the ladder:

- **Early (feed now, calm baby):** stirring, mouth opening, turning his head toward touch (rooting), hands to mouth.
- **Mid (feed soon, getting fussy):** stretching, restless movement, more insistent hand-sucking.
- **Late (feed immediately, will take longer to settle):** crying, agitated movement, turning red.

Waking a sleepy baby to feed in the first days is sometimes necessary — newborns who sleep 5 hours straight in week 1 may need waking every 2–3 hours until feeding is established and weight gain is on track. Ask the paediatrician when you can stop waking him.

## What's normal (the reassurance section)

Newborns do a lot of things that look alarming and aren't:

- **Sleep: 14–17 hours a day**, in 2–4 hour fragments. He has no day/night sense yet. (Weeks to develop. Sorry.)
- **Cluster feeding:** long stretches of near-constant feeding, usually in the evening. It builds milk supply and it's exhausting and it's normal.
- **Startle (Moro) reflex:** arms fling out, then pull in, like he's falling — triggered by noise or movement. Fades over the first months.
- **Hiccups and sneezes:** constant, harmless. Sneezing clears his nose; it doesn't mean he's sick.
- **Noisy breathing and grunting:** newborn airways are narrow and floppy. Occasional congestion sounds are normal; persistent laboured breathing is not (chapter 10).
- **Weight loss then regain:** losing up to **~10% of birth weight** in the first days is expected. He should be back to birth weight by **~2 weeks**. The paediatrician tracks this — it's the main reason for the early weigh-ins.
- **Jaundice, days 2–4:** yellow tinge to skin and eyes, very common. The paediatrician checks bilirubin levels. Most cases are mild and clear on their own; feeding frequently helps flush it. Call promptly if the yellow spreads downward (face → chest → belly → legs), he becomes very sleepy and hard to wake for feeds, or feeds poorly — those are escalation signs, not wait-and-see signs.
- **Pee/poop counts:** by day 5, 6+ pale wet diapers and 3+ dirty ones per 24 hours (see the table above).
- **Umbilical stump:** falls off in 1–3 weeks (see cord care above).
- **Soft spot (fontanelle):** the diamond-shaped soft spot on his head is supposed to be there. Don't be afraid of it — gentle touching during baths is fine.

## Taking his temperature

You need a digital thermometer and you need to know how to use it before you need it.

- **Rectal is the most accurate method in newborns.** Armpit readings run low and are unreliable for decision-making. Learn the rectal technique from the nurse in the ward — it's quick, it's safe when done as taught, and it's the number the doctor will trust.
- **Fever in a baby under 3 months — ≥38.0°C (100.4°F) — is a hospital visit, immediately.** Not "call in the morning." Not "wait and see." Newborns can get seriously ill very fast and they can't show it the way older babies do. This is the single most important threshold in this chapter: **38°C + under 3 months = go now.** Full detail on this and every other red flag is in chapter 10.
