# Chapter 2 — Birth Partner Crash Course

*Read this in about 20 minutes. This is the chapter that answers your real question: what will labour actually look like, and what do I DO in it?*

> This chapter is education, not medical advice. Follow your gynae/midwife on the day. Markers **[confirm with TMC/your gynae]** mean: ask at the next appointment.

---

## The one-sentence job description

**You are her anchor, her advocate, and her memory.** She will be doing the hardest physical work of her life. You keep the room calm, you track what's happening, you speak up when she can't — and you don't need to fix anything. Presence is the job.

---

## The stages of labour — in partner terms

### Stage 1a — Early (latent) labour: the long beginning

**What's happening:** Contractions start, cervix begins to thin and open (0–~4 cm). This can last *hours to a full day or more* in a first labour.

**What you'll see:** She'll be uncomfortable but functional — walking, talking between contractions, maybe annoyed it's taking so long. Contractions are irregular and mild-ish at first.

**Your job:**
- **Stay home as long as sensible.** Early labour at home is calmer than early labour in a hospital bed. Time contractions (5-1-1 rule from Chapter 1), keep her fed and hydrated, help her rest between them.
- Distraction is medicine now: walk, shower, TV, normal conversation.
- **Don't rush to the hospital.** Admitting too early is linked to more interventions. Call TMC for guidance; triage sending you home (CASE ⑨) is normal.
- Eat and sleep yourself when you can. This may be a marathon and you going in exhausted helps no one.

### Stage 1b — Active labour: the real work

**What's happening:** Contractions get longer, stronger, closer together (~3–4 min apart). Cervix opens ~4–7 cm. This is usually when you're admitted.

**What you'll see and hear (honestly):** She'll get quieter and turn inward. She may moan or vocalize through contractions — this is good, not distress. She might shake, feel nauseous, vomit. Her focus narrows to one contraction at a time; she may stop answering questions normally. All normal.

**Your job:**
- **One contraction at a time.** Don't talk about "how much longer." Just: "This one. Breathe with me."
- Be her **metronome**: breathe audibly with her, slow and low. She'll unconsciously match you.
- **Counter-pressure and hip squeeze** (see comfort measures below) — these are your highest-value physical tools.
- **Guard the room:** dim lights, quiet voices, minimal interruptions. Ask staff to cluster their checks. Privacy isn't a luxury — adrenaline (from stress, bright lights, strangers) slows labour; oxytocin (from safety, calm, darkness) drives it.
- **Track:** contraction timing, when waters broke (colour!), fluid intake, last time she peed (a full bladder can slow things). You're her memory.

### Transition — the storm before pushing (~7–10 cm)

**What's happening:** The hardest, shortest phase — often 30 min to 2 hours. Contractions come almost on top of each other.

**What you'll see and hear:** This is the famous **"I can't do this"** moment. She may say she wants to quit, wants the epidural NOW, wants to go home. She may shake hard, vomit, sweat, cry. She might get suddenly irritable with *you* specifically.

**Read this twice: this is the most normal thing in labour. "I can't do it" in transition almost always means she is nearly done.** It does not mean she actually wants to abandon the plan — it means she's in transition.

**Your job:**
- **Do not take the words at face value; do take her seriously.** Calm, short phrases: "You're doing it. This is transition — it means you're almost there." Repeat. Don't argue, don't explain.
- If she asks for the epidural here: this is her call, always (see Chapter 3). But know that transition is usually too fast for one to take effect anyway — the midwife can advise in the moment.
- Stay physically close. Eye contact. Low voice. You are the calmest person in the room.

### Stage 2 — Pushing

**What's happening:** Fully dilated, urge to push. Can last minutes to 2+ hours in a first birth.

**What you'll see:** Intense effort, sounds you have never heard from her, possibly poop (totally normal, nobody cares, the midwife wipes it without comment). Then — the head crowns, and very fast, there's a baby.

**Your job:**
- Hold a leg, support her back, whatever position the midwife suggests. Be where she can see you.
- Between pushes: water, ice chips, "rest now, next one soon."
- **When the baby is out:** if all is well, he goes straight onto her chest (golden hour — deep dive in ../weeks/week-32/reading.md). Your job: witness it, keep the room quiet, take the photo if she wants one. Delayed cord clamping is common practice — [confirm with TMC/your gynae] what their standard is.

### Stage 3 — Placenta

**What's happening:** Minutes after birth, mild contractions deliver the placenta. Usually quick and anticlimactic.

**Your job:** Stay with her. The midwife handles this. If there's heavy bleeding afterward, that's the team's emergency to manage — your job is to stay at her head, stay calm, let them work (CASE ⑭ covers the details).

## Comfort measures — your toolbox

You don't need all of these. Learn three well: **breathing with her, counter-pressure, and position changes.**

- **Positions:** Upright and mobile beats lying flat. Walking, swaying on you, hands-and-knees, birth ball, squatting, side-lying in bed. Suggest a change every 30–45 min in active labour — movement helps the baby descend.
- **Breathing:** Slow in through the nose, long out through the mouth. You do it audibly; she follows. In transition, short panting breaths can help her *not* push before she's ready.
- **Counter-pressure:** Heel of your hand pressed hard into her lower back/sacrum during each contraction. Ask "harder or softer?" — she'll tell you. This is the single most effective partner technique for back pain.
- **Hip squeeze:** Hands on both hip bones, pressing inward during contractions. Especially good when counter-pressure isn't enough.
- **Water:** Warm shower on her back, or the bath if TMC's delivery suite has one [confirm with TMC/your gynae]. Water is genuinely effective pain relief.
- **Vocalization:** Low moaning = good (keeps the throat/jaw open, which is linked to the pelvis staying open). High-pitched screaming = tension; gently guide her voice lower: "low and deep, like this."
- **Environment:** Dim, quiet, private. Bring a playlist if she wants one. Keep your phone on silent — nobody needs your group chat lighting up mid-labour.
- **Touch:** Some women want constant touch; some want *nobody touching them* in transition. Ask early, re-ask later. Both are fine.

## Timing contractions — refresher

Start when they feel real. For each contraction record: **start time, how long it lasted, and the gap to the next start.** Use an app or a notes page — don't rely on memory at 3am. Time for at least an hour to confirm the pattern before calling. What the midwife wants to hear: "Contractions every 4 minutes, lasting a minute, for the last hour. Waters broke at 2am, clear."

## BRAIN — your decision framework

When anyone proposes an intervention (induction, epidural, breaking waters, c-section), run **BRAIN** — out loud, calmly:

- **B — Benefits:** What does this gain us?
- **R — Risks:** What are the downsides?
- **A — Alternatives:** What else could we try first? (Position change? More time? Water?)
- **I — Intuition:** What does *she* feel about it? (Ask her, even mid-labour — a nod counts.)
- **N — Nothing:** What happens if we do nothing for 30 minutes and reassess?

Then the magic question: **"Is this an emergency, or do we have 10 minutes to think?"** Emergencies get instant yes. Everything else gets BRAIN. (Full guide to each intervention is Chapter 3.)

## Talking to the midwife/gynae — scripts

Your tone: **respectful, direct, on her team.** You're not fighting the staff — good advocacy sounds like partnership.

- **Stating her preferences early:** "Just so you know — she's hoping for unmedicated. We're flexible if things change, but we'd like to try position changes and time before interventions."
- **Asking for a minute:** "Can we have a few minutes to talk this through together?" (Almost always granted unless it's a true emergency.)
- **Asking for alternatives:** "Before we do that — is there anything else we could try first?"
- **When she's non-verbal:** "She's finding it hard to talk right now — can I answer for her?" Then check with her: a nod, a squeeze of the hand.
- **When something feels off:** "I'm a bit worried about [X] — can you help me understand what's happening?" Calm, specific, non-accusatory. Escalate firmly if dismissed and you're genuinely concerned — you know her baseline better than anyone on shift.

What NOT to do: don't argue philosophy with staff mid-labour, don't quote the internet at the midwife, don't make it you-vs-them. The fastest way to get what she wants is to be the calm, reasonable person the staff wants to help.

## The unmedicated mindset — and what flexibility really means

You're planning an unmedicated birth because she wants to experience it that way. That's a good reason, and the preparation in this chapter serves it. Two honest companions to that plan:

1. **Pain has a purpose here, and she can do hard things.** Every contraction is doing work. The plan is built on that trust.
2. **Flexibility is not failure. The plan serves her, not the other way around.** If she asks for the epidural — once, clearly — you get it. No guilt, no debate, no "but the plan." Agree on this sentence together *now*, before labour: *"We'll try unmedicated, but if you ask for the epidural even once, we get it."* A birth that ends with interventions and a healthy mom and baby is not a failed birth. It's a birth.

## Comfort kit — pack for yourself too

The hospital bag list (PART 1) covers her and the baby. **Your** delivery-room kit, separate small bag:

- Phone + **long charger cable** (sockets are never where you want them)
- Snacks that don't smell (granola bars, bananas), water bottle, coins/cash for vending machines
- A change of clothes + deodorant (you may be there 12+ hours)
- The PART 0 page (numbers, insurance) — printed, not just on your phone
- Lip balm for her (hospitals are dry; she'll thank you mid-labour)
- This course's Chapter 2 on your phone for the 3am "what was transition again" moment

## Common partner mistakes (learn from other dads)

1. **Asking "are you okay?" every 5 minutes.** She's not okay, she's in labour — the question just reminds her. Replace with presence: hand on her back, breathing with her, water offered between contractions.
2. **Narrating the dilation number like a sports score.** "You're 7cm!" can backfire if the next check is still 7cm. Let the midwife report; you report encouragement.
3. **Leaving to "give her space" without telling anyone.** If you must step out (eat, air), tell the midwife and be back in 10 minutes. Disappearing reads as abandonment mid-labour.
4. **Making decisions *for* her when she's still capable.** Offer, don't order: "Do you want counter-pressure?" beats grabbing her hips.
5. **Forgetting to eat.** A faint partner is a liability. Eat before you go in, snack during early labour.
6. **Taking transition personally.** "Don't touch me!" / "I hate you!" in transition is the hormones talking. Smile later; stay now.
