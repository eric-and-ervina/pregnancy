# Chapter 8 — You, the New Dad: Surviving the First Month

> Read this in ~15 minutes. Nobody in the hospital hands fathers a manual. This is yours.

---

## 1. The sleep shift system — set this up before the birth

Sleep deprivation is the root cause of most first-month problems: fights, tears, feeling insane. You cannot out-tough it. You need a *system*.

The principle: **one parent gets an unbroken 5–6 hour block.** "Sleep when the baby sleeps" gives you 45-minute naps. A shift system gives you real sleep.

A concrete example (adjust the hours to your lives):

| Shift | Who's on duty | Who sleeps |
|---|---|---|
| **9pm – 2am** | You: all feeds, all diaper changes, all settling | Her: sleeps in the other room, earplugs in |
| **2am – 7am** | Her: feeds (breastfeeds directly) | You: sleep in the other room, earplugs in |

The rules that make it work:

- The off-shift person **sleeps in the other room with earplugs**. Not on the couch in the living room "in case." Real sleep. This is non-negotiable — couch-dozing is how both parents end up broken.
- The on-duty person handles *everything* — feed, burp, change, settle, cry. You only wake the sleeper for a genuine emergency.
- If she's breastfeeding and you're on shift, use expressed milk from the fridge or freezer (labelled with date/time). Warm it gently, never microwave.

**Why this matters most for her:** protected sleep is the single highest-leverage thing you do for her recovery (Chapter 7). A mother who gets 5 unbroken hours is a different person from one who gets eight 45-minute fragments.

## 2. First-two-weeks survival rules

1. **Lower every bar.** The house can be messy. Meals can be simple. You can wear the same shirt twice. Standards are for week 12.
2. **Say yes to help.** Every offer — food, laundry, a 30-minute walk with the baby — is a gift. Accept it. The only help to decline is help that comes with opinions about how you should parent.
3. **You are a team of two first.** Decisions about the baby, the house, visitors — you two decide. Grandparents advise; you two decide. (Section 5 is about your parents specifically.)
4. **Don't make big decisions.** Not about jobs, not about moving, not about money. Sleep-deprived brains are terrible at strategy.
5. **Feed yourself.** Skipped meals make everything worse. Keep one-handed food stocked: bananas, bread, nuts, whatever works.

## 3. Your emotions — dad blues are real

Here's something nobody told most new dads: **paternal postnatal depression is real.** About **5–10% of fathers** experience it, usually peaking 3–6 months in — but the low mood, anxiety, and stress start much earlier.

What it looks like in a dad:

- **Irritability and anger** — snapping at small things, then feeling guilty about it.
- **Withdrawal** — working longer hours, hiding in the bathroom, emotional distance from her and the baby.
- **Anxiety** — a constant low hum of worry about money, the baby's breathing, whether you're failing.
- **Feeling useless** — especially while she's the one feeding and recovering. You're not useless, but the feeling is real.

This is not weakness, and it's not just "being tired" — though exhaustion makes everything worse.

**What to do:**
- Tell someone. Her, a friend, your GP. Naming it out loud is half the work.
- The same helplines apply to you: **SOS 1-767** (24h), **IMH 6389 2222** (24h), **mindline.sg 1771**. Call them. They've heard it before.
- If low mood, anxiety, or irritability persist beyond two weeks, or you have thoughts of harming yourself — **see your GP or call IMH 6389 2222 now.**

The baby-blues vs PPD distinction in Chapter 7 applies to you too, just with different outward signs.

## 4. Asking for help — be specific

"Let me know if you need anything" gets no reply. Specific asks get results.

Instead of:
> "We could use some help."

Say:
> "Could you bring us dinner on Tuesday? Anything without seafood."
> "Could you come hold the baby Saturday morning so we can sleep?"
> "Could you grab these groceries on your way over?"

**Vague asks die. Specific asks get answered.** Keep a short list in your phone of things people can do, so when someone offers, you hand them something concrete.

## 5. Your parents are flying in — help AND guests

This is a uniquely Singapore-Indonesian situation and it needs handling with care. Your parents are coming from Jakarta out of love. They will be **enormously helpful** — and they are also **guests in a small 2-bedroom HDB during the most intense weeks of your lives.** Both things are true at the same time.

**Give them concrete roles before they arrive.** People help best when they know their job:

- **Cooking** — to the confinement meal plan (eric-and-ervina/confinement repo). Send them the plan *before* they fly. This is the single most valuable contribution.
- **Holding the baby** so you and Ervina sleep — including being "on shift" during the day so you both get a daytime nap block.
- **Laundry, cleaning, groceries** — the invisible labour that never stops.

**Set kind boundaries early — in the first 48 hours, not week 3:**

- **Rest hours are real.** Agree on quiet hours (e.g., 9pm–8am) when the household sleeps and the living room stays dark. Their room is their retreat — knock before entering, and extend them the same courtesy.
- **Decision authority stays with you two.** "We appreciate the advice, but we've decided to do X." Say it warmly, say it once, don't reopen the vote.
- **No unannounced visitors.** Every guest goes through you. The house is not open for drop-ins during confinement month.
- **Confinement rules are her call, not theirs.** If their traditions differ from hers on what she should eat or do — hers win. Full stop.

Have this conversation **before** they arrive, on a calm video call, framed as love: "We want you to enjoy the grandbaby, not be exhausted — here's how we'll set it up." Boundaries set early with warmth beat boundaries set late with resentment.

## 6. Couple communication under sleep deprivation

You will be short with each other. That's the sleep, not the marriage. Two tools:

**The 10-minute evening check-in.** Same time each night (before your shift or after hers ends). Three questions each:
1. What was hardest today?
2. What do you need tomorrow?
3. One thing the other person did that helped.

Ten minutes. Phones down. This tiny ritual catches problems when they're small.

**"I" statements and fast repair.** When you're snappy (not if — when):
> "I'm sorry, I was short earlier. I'm running on three hours. What I meant was ___."

Repair fast, don't defend. Sleep-deprived pride is the enemy.

What not to do: keep a tally of who slept more, who fed more, who changed more. The tally always lies and it always hurts. You're both running on empty. Assume good intent.

## 7. Work logistics — do this the week before the birth

The Labour Day Guide covers this in [Parts 8.4 and 9.2](../program/LABOUR-DAY-GUIDE.md) — here's the condensed version:

- **The night of the birth:** text your boss. Short and factual: "My wife delivered at [time]. Mother and baby well. I'll be offline [X days], reachable for genuine emergencies."
- **Formal HR notice within 1–2 working days.** Know the procedure *now* — don't be reading the employee handbook at 2am with a newborn.
- **This week, read your contract.** Know exactly what *your* employer gives you: paternity leave (government-paid paternity leave requires a citizen child, which doesn't apply — Chapter 9 has the details), compassionate leave, annual leave. Write the numbers down. If your company offers more than the statutory minimum, claim every day.
- **Set expectations early:** "I'll be slower than usual for the first month, fully back after." Underpromise, overdeliver.

---

## 8. Lower the bar everywhere

- **Training:** Program v6.0 will survive a pause. Sleep > squats. Maintenance mode — a couple of light sessions a week if you feel like it, zero guilt if you don't. Come back when the baby sleeps through the night (months away; don't think about it).
- **Social life:** declines are fine, rainchecks are fine, disappearing for a month is fine. Real friends understand.
- **Inbox:** email can wait. Text messages can wait. Everything can wait except her, the baby, and the paperwork in Chapter 9.

---

## 9. When to call your own doctor

You don't get a 6-week check. You get your own judgment. Call your GP if:

- Low mood, anxiety, or irritability that doesn't lift after ~2 weeks.
- You're drinking or using anything to cope with the stress.
- Thoughts of harming yourself — call **SOS 1-767** or **IMH 6389 2222** immediately, 24 hours.
- Any physical symptom you're ignoring because "it's not important right now" — chest pain, persistent anything. You're no good to them if you collapse.
