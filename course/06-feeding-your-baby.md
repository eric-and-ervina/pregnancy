# Chapter 6 — Feeding Your Baby

> Read this in ~15 minutes. How breastfeeding works, your actual job list as the support person, and how to do formula safely — whichever way your family goes. Deliberately neutral: your decision is still open, and this chapter is education, not medical advice.

## A note before we start

You and Ervina haven't decided yet: full breastfeeding, partial, or a mix. That's fine — plenty of families decide after the first week, once they see how things go. This chapter prepares you for all three, because the useful knowledge overlaps more than you'd expect:

- If you mix, you need both halves.

## How breastfeeding works

**Colostrum comes first.** For the first 2–4 days she makes colostrum — thick, yellowish, in tiny amounts: teaspoons per feed, not ounces. This is not "milk not coming in." It is the entire design: colostrum is concentrated nutrition and antibodies, and his stomach is the size of a marble on day 1, a walnut by day 3, an egg by day 10. Small amounts are exactly right.

**Mature milk arrives around day 2–4** — often with a feeling of fullness or heaviness. Her breasts may feel engorged and tender for a day or two (see engorgement below).

**Supply = demand.** Milk production runs on removal: the more milk leaves the breast (by feeding or expressing), the more the body makes. This is why frequent early feeds matter — they're placing the order for the weeks ahead. It's also why long gaps in the first weeks can quietly reduce supply.

**8–12 feeds per 24 hours is normal.** Newborns feed every 2–3 hours around the clock, including at night. Some feeds take 10 minutes, some take 40. Cluster feeding — feeding almost constantly for hours, usually in the evening — is normal in the first weeks and it builds supply. It is not a sign she's "not making enough."

**Feeding cues** are covered in chapter 5 — early cues (rooting, hands to mouth) are your green light. Catch them early and the feed goes smoothly; wait for crying and everyone has a harder time.

### Signs feeding is going well (the first weeks)

- 8–12 feeds per 24 hours
- Audible swallowing during feeds (not just sucking)
- He seems content and sleepy after most feeds
- By day 5: 6+ pale wet diapers and 3+ dirty diapers per 24 hours
- Weight back to birth weight by ~2 weeks, then steady gain

If several of these aren't happening — especially poor weight gain or very few wet diapers — that's a call to the lactation consultant or paediatrician, promptly. Not a reason to panic; usually a fixable problem.

## Your support role — the real job list

Here's the part most preparation material skips: if she breastfeeds, *she* feeds the baby and *you* run everything else. This is not a side role. It's the job that makes breastfeeding possible.

1. **Water and food within her reach, every feed.** She will be thirsty mid-feed, every time. Put a big bottle of water and a snack where she sits *before* she starts. Do not make her ask.
2. **Pillows and positioning.** Bring pillows, adjust them, get the footstool. Her shoulders, neck and back will ache from hunching — good positioning prevents weeks of pain.
3. **Burp the baby.** After every feed, he's yours: upright on your shoulder or sitting on your lap, gentle pats or rubs on the back for a few minutes. Sometimes the burp comes in 30 seconds, sometimes never — if he's comfortable and not fussy, you're done.
4. **Track feeds and diapers.** App or notebook by the nursing spot: which side, what time, how long, wet/dirty diapers. Sleep-deprived parents cannot remember this. The paediatrician will ask, and your log is the answer.
5. **Protect her rest.** She feeds and sleeps. Everything else — diapers, laundry, cooking, cleaning, the HDB, messages, visitors — is yours. This is non-negotiable in the first weeks.
6. **Handle visitors.** You are the bouncer. Short visits, no visits during feed times unless she wants company, and anyone who shows up with a cold doesn't hold the baby.
7. **Be the one who calls for help.** If feeding hurts her, if he's not gaining, if she's crying at 3am convinced it's not working — you call the lactation consultant. She should not have to research her own rescue at 3am.

**TMC has lactation consultants.** Ask for one in the ward *before* discharge — even if feeding seems fine, a 15-minute observed feed with a professional catches small problems early. This is one of the highest-value asks of the whole hospital stay.

### The night-shift system (concrete example)

Nights are where families break down. Agree on a system *before* the baby comes. One that works:

- **9pm–2am: your shift.** If you're combo-feeding or she has expressed milk, you do full feeds with a bottle while she sleeps a solid block. If she's exclusively breastfeeding, you still do everything around the feed: you bring him to her, you change him, you burp and resettle him after, she goes straight back to sleep. Her awake time per night feed: 20–30 minutes. Yours: 45–60.
- **2am–morning: her shift**, or split it the same way in reverse.
- **Rule:** the person off-shift sleeps in the other room with earplugs if needed. A solid 4–5 hour block of sleep keeps a human functional. Two people getting 2-hour fragments keeps nobody functional.

When her parents/your parents arrive from Jakarta, fold them into this system — a grandparent on the 9pm–2am shift is worth more than any gift.

## Latch basics and spotting trouble

A good latch: his mouth open wide, lips flanged outward (like a fish, not tucked in), chin pressed into the breast, nose free or just touching, and *more of the areola visible above his mouth than below*. You should hear rhythmic suck-swallow-breathe, not just quick shallow sucks.

**Spotting trouble — call a lactation consultant if:**

- Pain beyond the first 30 seconds of latch that doesn't ease as the feed goes on. Some tenderness in the first days is common; sharp, toe-curling pain or pain through the whole feed is not.
- Clicking or smacking sounds (he's losing suction).
- Cracked, bleeding, or blistered nipples.
- He feeds constantly but seems unsatisfied, has too few wet diapers, or isn't gaining weight.
- She dreads feeds.

Nearly all of these are fixable — positioning tweaks, a tongue-tie check, a feeding plan. The mistake is suffering through them for weeks. Early help works; late help still works but with more misery in between.

### Positions (brief)

- **Cradle:** his head in the crook of her arm, tummy to tummy. The classic; harder with a sleepy newborn.
- **Cross-cradle:** opposite arm supports his head and neck — more control, good for the early weeks and for latching practice.
- **Football (clutch):** him tucked under her arm like a rugby ball, head in her hand. Great after a C-section (keeps weight off the belly) and for small babies.
- **Side-lying:** both lying on their sides, tummy to tummy. The night-feed position — but **only on a firm flat bed with no pillows/blankets near him**, and she must be awake enough to stay aware. If there's any chance she'll fall asleep, feed sitting up instead. [Confirm the safe side-lying guidance with TMC/your paediatrician.]

## Burping technique

After every feed, and mid-feed if he pulls off fussy:

- **Over the shoulder:** him upright against your chest, your hand supporting his head and neck, gentle pats or firm rubs on his back.
- **Sitting on your lap:** lean him slightly forward, one hand on his chest and jaw (not his throat), pat with the other.
- Give it a few minutes. Some babies burp immediately, some need position changes, some barely burp at all and are fine. If he's content and not fussy, you're done.

Spit-up after feeds is normal in small amounts — newborn stomach valves are loose. Projectile vomiting, green (bile) vomit, or vomit with blood is not normal — chapter 10.

## Engorgement and sore nipples

**Engorgement** — when the milk comes in around day 2–4, her breasts can become hard, hot, painful, and the nipples flatten, which makes latching harder. What helps: feed frequently (don't skip), warm compress or warm shower before a feed to help flow, cold compress between feeds for the pain, and hand-expressing or pumping just enough to soften the areola so he can latch. It usually eases within a day or two as supply calibrates.

**Sore nipples** — early tenderness is common; cracked or bleeding nipples mean the latch needs fixing (see above — lactation consultant, early). Between feeds: a thin smear of her own milk left to air-dry, or medical-grade lanolin. Change nursing pads when damp. What *doesn't* help: "toughening" them, or pushing through worsening pain without getting the latch checked.

## Pumping and expressing basics

**When:** to relieve engorgement, to build a small stash for your night shift or outings, when she's separated from the baby, or when returning to work (she's entitled to 12 weeks maternity leave — see the Labour Day Guide PART 9.2 — and pumping is how many mothers continue after that).

**How:** hand expression works and is worth learning (ask the lactation consultant to demo — it's a skill, not intuitive). Electric pumps are faster for regular use; hospital-grade rentals exist if she needs serious volume early.

**Storage — the standard "rule of 4s" guideline:** freshly expressed milk keeps about **4 hours at room temperature**, about **4 days in the fridge**, and about **6 months in a freezer** (longer in a deep freezer — [confirm with your paediatrician]). Store in small amounts (60–120 ml) in clean breast-milk bags or bottles, label with date and time, cool fresh milk in the fridge before adding to already-frozen milk. Thaw in the fridge overnight or under warm running water — never microwave (hot spots scald, and it destroys protective components). Swirl, don't shake. Use thawed milk within 24 hours; never refreeze.

## Formula: safe preparation

If your family uses formula — exclusively or in the mix — the safety rules are specific and worth following exactly. Powdered formula is not sterile; it can contain bacteria (notably *Cronobacter*), which is why the preparation steps exist.

**Safe prep, step by step (WHO guidance):**

1. Wash hands. Sterilise bottles, teats, caps, and any utensils (see below).
2. **Boil fresh water and let it cool for no more than 30 minutes** — you want water at **≥70°C** to kill bacteria in the powder. (Boiled-then-fully-cooled water doesn't do this; the heat is the point.)
3. Pour the hot water into the sterilised bottle first, then add the exact number of scoops — level scoops, not heaped, using the scoop from that tin. Too much powder strains his kidneys; too little underfeeds him. Never dilute to "make it last."
4. Shake/mix, then **cool the bottle rapidly** under cold running water to feeding temperature.
5. Test on the inside of your wrist — warm, not hot.

**Sterilising:** until he's older ([confirm the age to stop with your paediatrician] — commonly until around 3–6 months, longer in some guidance), sterilise bottles and teats before each use: boiling for 5 minutes, a steam steriliser, or cold-water sterilising tablets all work.

**Paced bottle feeding:** hold him semi-upright, keep the bottle horizontal so he has to work for the milk (not tipped so it pours), pause every few minutes, let him decide when he's done. This prevents overfeeding and keeps bottle feeds closer to the rhythm of breastfeeding — it matters especially if you're combo-feeding, so he doesn't develop a strong bottle preference.

**Storage of made bottles:** use within **2 hours at room temperature**. Refrigerate immediately if not using; use within 24 hours. Discard unfinished formula after the feed — saliva introduces bacteria, so a half-finished bottle doesn't keep. Never microwave to reheat (same hot-spot problem as breast milk).

**Choosing formula:** standard cow's-milk infant formula is the default unless the paediatrician advises otherwise. Don't switch brands repeatedly because of normal fussiness — and don't use toddler milks, condensed milk, or homemade recipes for a newborn. If cost is a concern, that's a conversation with the paediatrician, not a reason to dilute.

## Combo-feeding: the mix works

Many families do both — breastfeeds plus some expressed milk or formula, or formula top-ups after breastfeeds. It works. A few practical notes:

- In the first 2–3 weeks, while supply is establishing, frequent breast emptying matters most — talk to the lactation consultant before adding regular formula top-ups so it doesn't quietly reduce supply.
- Paced bottle feeding (above) keeps him from developing a strong flow preference.
- "Nipple confusion" is overstated as a concept, but flow preference is real — a fast bottle teat can make a baby impatient at the breast. Slower-flow teats help.
- There is no purity test. A baby who gets some breast milk and some formula is a fed baby. Fed is the goal.

## When to call for help

- **Lactation consultant** (TMC has them — get the contact before discharge): latch pain, cracked nipples, engorgement that isn't easing, worries about supply, baby not gaining.
- **Paediatrician:** poor weight gain, too few wet diapers by day 5, persistent vomiting, blood in stool, jaundice spreading or baby too sleepy to feed.
- **GP / your own doctor:** for her — fever, worsening breast pain with redness (possible mastitis — needs prompt treatment, and she can usually keep feeding), or feeling persistently low (chapter 7 covers the emotional side).
- **Emergency (995) / TMC +65 6250 2222:** any red flag from chapter 10.
