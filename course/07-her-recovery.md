# Chapter 7 — Her Recovery: The First 6 Weeks

> Read this in ~15 minutes. This is education, not medical advice — her doctor has the final say on all of it.

She just did the hardest physical thing a human can do. Your job now: make recovery as frictionless as possible. This chapter is the map of what's happening to her body, what's normal, what's not, and exactly how you help.

---

## 1. Lochia — the bleeding

After birth, her uterus sheds blood, mucus, and tissue. This is called **lochia**. It is not a period, and it will last **4–6 weeks**.

The normal progression:

| Stage | What | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| **Lochia rubra** | Bright or dark red, like a heavy period | Days 1–4 |
| **Lochia serosa** | Pinkish-brown, lighter flow | Days 4–10 |
| **Lochia alba** | Yellowish-white, mostly spotting | Days 10–42 |

**What's normal:**
- Clots the size of a coin in the first day or two.
- Flow gets heavier when she's been active (breastfeeding and walking both trigger surges) and lighter at rest.
- A slightly metallic or earthy smell.

**What's NOT normal — call her doctor or go to TMC immediately:**
- Soaking a full pad in an hour, for two hours in a row.
- Clots larger than a golf ball.
- Bleeding that was lightening and then suddenly becomes heavy and bright red again.
- Foul-smelling discharge (sweet/rotten, not just "bloody").
- Fever of 38°C or above.

**Your role:** Buy the supplies before the birth — heavy-duty maternity pads (not tampons; nothing goes inside the vagina for 6 weeks), and change them every few hours. If you're unsure whether the bleeding looks wrong, you call. Don't ask her to decide whether she's bleeding "enough" to warrant a call. That's your call to make.

## 2. Afterpains

As her uterus shrinks back to size, it contracts — these cramps are called **afterpains**. They're real pain, not discomfort.

- Worst on **days 2–3**, then ease over the first week or so.
- They are **worse during breastfeeding** (the same hormone, oxytocin, causes both milk let-down and uterine cramping). If she's nursing, she'll feel them flare with every feed. This is normal and actually good — it means her body is working.
- Worse with each subsequent baby. First babies are the mildest round.

**Your role:** Keep paracetamol available (check with her doctor first). A warm compress or a warm shower helps. The most useful thing you can do is say "that's the oxytocin doing its job" instead of looking worried when she winces — confidence is contagious.

---

## 3. Perineal care (if she delivered vaginally)

The perineum — the tissue between the vagina and anus — stretches, may tear, or may be cut (episiotomy). It will be sore for 1–2 weeks. If she has stitches, they **dissolve on their own** — no removal appointment needed.

The care routine:

1. **Peri bottle** — fill with warm water and squeeze-rinse after every toilet visit. Buy one before the birth.
2. **Pat dry, never wipe.** Front to back. Use a soft cloth or dab with tissue.
3. **Ice packs** — wrapped in cloth, 15 minutes at a time, for the first day or two.
4. **Witch-hazel pads** — lay them on the maternity pad for soothing.
5. **Sitz bath** — a shallow basin of warm water she sits in, 10–15 minutes, once or twice a day. Ask her doctor when to start.

**Haemorrhoids** are common after vaginal birth (pushing does that). Witch hazel, warm water, and not straining on the toilet. They usually resolve in a week or two. If one is extremely painful or thrombosed (hard purple lump), call the doctor.

**Bladder and bowels:**
- First pee after delivery can feel impossible or burn. Totally normal. Warm water over the area (peri bottle) helps the stream start.
- First poop is the one everyone dreads. It will happen, it will be fine. Stool softeners if her doctor prescribed them, drink plenty of water, fibre from the confinement food plan.
- She should pee within 6 hours of delivery. If she can't, tell the nurse — a full bladder after birth is a genuine problem.

**Your role:** Keep the peri bottle clean and filled. Make the sitz bath water. Handle the washing of pads/soaked items without being asked. Never, ever joke about her bathroom situation.

## 4. If it comes to a c-section

Unmedicated natural birth is the plan, but plans change. If she delivers by c-section:

- **Get her up on day 1.** It hurts, and it's still important — walking prevents blood clots. Be her human crutch.
- **Wound care:** keep it clean and dry, watch for increasing redness, swelling, warmth, or drainage. Follow her doctor's exact instructions on dressings.
- **Lifting limit:** roughly the baby's weight, and nothing heavier, for the first weeks.
- **Driving and exercise:** only when her doctor clears it — not before.
- Pain control is not optional heroism. Take the meds on schedule.

---

## 5. Rest rules (the first two weeks)

Her body is repairing tissue damage. Treat it like a real injury, because it is one.

- **No lifting beyond the baby.**
- **No hosting.** Visitors don't get hosted — if they come, they serve. Better yet: limit visitors in week one to almost nobody. (More on this in Chapter 8, because your parents are flying in.)
- **Sleep when the baby sleeps** is half-useful advice — the useful half is: *you* protect her sleep so she can do it (Chapter 8 has the shift system).
- Stairs are fine if taken slowly; marathon housework is not.

---

## 6. Nutrition — the confinement food plan

Her body is rebuilding blood and tissue. She needs warm, nourishing food — lots of protein, iron, and fluids, especially if breastfeeding.

The full food plan lives where it belongs: the **eric-and-ervina/confinement** repo (meal-plan.md and the herbs shopping list). Don't wing this with takeaway for three weeks — the confinement plan exists for exactly this job. Your role is to make the cooking happen: you cook, your parents cook to the plan, or you arrange help. The point is she eats warm meals three times a day without having to think about it.

One thing to note from the confinement repo's herb guide: avoid **麦芽 (barley malt)** in her food and teas — it's flagged as 回乳 (milk-suppressing), which works against breastfeeding.

## 7. The 6-week check

This appointment is non-negotiable. Her doctor will check:

- Uterus and pelvic floor recovery.
- Stitches / perineum / c-section wound.
- **Screening for postnatal depression** — often the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale, a short questionnaire.
- Whether she's cleared for exercise and intimacy.

Put it in the calendar now, before the birth. Not "we'll book it later." Now.

---

## 8. Her emotions — baby blues vs postnatal depression

This is the part first-time dads underestimate most. Read it carefully.

**Baby blues** — days 3–10, roughly 80% of new mothers:
- Weepy, moody, anxious, overwhelmed. Comes and goes in waves.
- Caused by the hormone crash (oestrogen and progesterone fall off a cliff after delivery), sleep deprivation, and the sheer shock of it all.
- **It passes on its own** within about two weeks.

**Postnatal depression (PPD)** — roughly 1 in 7 mothers:
- Same symptoms but **deeper, longer, and not lifting** — persistent sadness or emptiness, inability to bond with the baby, intrusive thoughts (including harm thoughts), severe anxiety, feeling like a failure as a mother.
- Can start in the first weeks or emerge over months.
- **This is a medical condition, not a character flaw.** It is treatable — therapy, medication, or both. It does not go away by trying harder.

**How to tell them apart:** blues lift within ~2 weeks; PPD stays or deepens. If she can't sleep *even when the baby sleeps*, if she feels nothing toward the baby, if she has thoughts of harming herself or the baby — that's past blues. Get professional help immediately.

**Where help is in Singapore:**
- **KKH and NUH** both run dedicated perinatal mental health programmes — subsidised, via referral from her OB, GP, or a polyclinic.
- **SOS (Samaritans of Singapore): 1-767**, 24 hours; WhatsApp CareText: **9151 1767**.
- **IMH Mental Health Helpline: 6389 2222**, 24 hours.
- **mindline.sg** or call **1771**.

**Your role is not to diagnose.** Your role is to notice, to say "I think this is more than the blues — let's call your doctor," and to make the call easy. Getting professional help early is strength, not failure. Tell her that, in those words.

## 9. How YOU help — the high-leverage list

1. **Protect her sleep.** This is the single highest-leverage thing you can do. Not nice words, not flowers — *sleep*. The shift system in Chapter 8 exists for this.
2. **Specific praise, often.** Not "you're doing great" — "the way you kept feeding through that cluster feed at 3am was incredible." Specific means you were actually paying attention.
3. **Watch without hovering.** Check in, don't interrogate. "How are you feeling, really?" once a day is worth ten "are you okay?"s.
4. **Take every feed you can.** Bottle of expressed milk or formula — if you're doing the 9pm–2am shift (Chapter 8), you feed and settle, she sleeps.
5. **Run the house.** Meals, laundry, bills, visitors, the baby's paperwork (Chapter 9). Every admin task you absorb is one more hour her body gets to recover.
6. **Don't keep score.** You'll both be wrecked. Nobody wins the exhaustion Olympics.

---

## 10. Intimacy — later

Cleared at the 6-week check, and only then, and only when she wants to. No rush, no pressure, no sulking. Her body, her timeline. Some couples take weeks beyond the 6-week mark. That's normal.
